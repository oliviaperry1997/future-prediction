# Great Lakes Water Level Decline

## Falsifiable Statement

The average water level of the Great Lakes will have declined by at least 2 meters by 2075 due to increased evaporation and reduced ice cover, altering the US-Canada water boundary and causing significant economic disruption to shipping and hydroelectric power.

## Reasoning

The Great Lakes contain roughly 21% of the world's surface freshwater and define a substantial portion of the US-Canada border. Climate models project that the Great Lakes basin will experience warmer temperatures, reduced ice cover, and increased evaporation over the coming decades, with lake-effect precipitation changes that may not compensate for evaporative losses. The 2012-2013 period saw record low water levels on Lakes Michigan and Huron (approximately 0.5 meters below the long-term average), providing a preview of the trend.

The mechanism is straightforward: warmer air and water temperatures increase the evaporation rate from the lake surfaces. Winter ice cover, which historically reduced winter evaporation, has been declining — the Great Lakes ice cover has decreased approximately 70% since the 1970s. With less ice, winter evaporation accelerates. If winter precipitation does not increase sufficiently to offset the combined evaporative losses, water levels will decline.

A 2-meter decline by 2075 would have significant consequences. The St. Lawrence Seaway, which enables ocean-going shipping to reach inland ports like Chicago, Detroit, and Cleveland, would face reduced draft depths, requiring lighter loads or dredging. Hydroelectric power generation at Niagara and the St. Lawrence River would be reduced. Shoreline property boundaries (defined by water level) would shift, creating legal and jurisdictional disputes. The US-Canada boundary in the lakes, defined by the international border running through the water, could become contested if the water level change significantly alters the shoreline.

This is a relatively low-confidence prediction because lake level projections are highly uncertain — they depend on precipitation changes (which climate models handle less reliably than temperature), ice cover dynamics, and the balance between evaporation and precipitation in a warming world. Some models project that increased winter precipitation could offset or even reverse evaporative losses, especially for Lake Superior (the deepest and coldest lake). The range of outcomes is wide, from minor declines to lake-level drops exceeding 2 meters.

## What Would Falsify This

- Great Lakes water levels remain within historical variability (within 1 meter of long-term average) through 2075
- Increased winter precipitation fully compensates for evaporative losses, maintaining or raising water levels
- Climate change mitigation (rapid decarbonization) significantly reduces warming in the basin
- Human intervention (engineering projects, diversions, or lake regulation changes) stabilizes water levels

## Confidence Criteria

- **HIGH:** Strong evidence across multiple domains, consistent with established trends
- **MEDIUM:** Plausible with supporting logic, some uncertainty
- **LOW:** Speculative but worth tracking; single line of evidence or contrarian signal

The LOW confidence reflects the significant uncertainty in Great Lakes water balance projections. The direction of change (warming → increased evaporation → potential decline) is physically well-founded, but the magnitude and timing are highly dependent on precipitation patterns that current climate models disagree on. This prediction is worth tracking because the implications for US-Canada relations, regional economies, and water resource management would be significant if it materializes, but the evidence base is currently insufficient for a higher confidence rating.
