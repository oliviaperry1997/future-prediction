# US Successor States Maintain Divergent Information Governance Regimes by 2045

**Confidence:** `= this.confidence` | **Target:** `= this.target_milestone`
**Domain:** `= this.domain` | **Doc Ref:** `= this.doc_ref`

## Falsifiable Statement

By 2045, at least three US successor states will maintain distinct information governance regimes — defined as different laws, platform regulations, content moderation standards, and information sovereignty requirements — that are mutually incompatible.

## Reasoning

The information ecosystem fragmentation driver (technology Driver 3) projects that successor states will use information sovereignty as a tool of identity formation and border enforcement. When a political entity is newly sovereign and its legitimacy is contested, controlling the information environment becomes a survival imperative — succession is not just about territory and economics but about narrative, about which version of history and identity citizens accept.

The three largest successor states are likely to develop three distinct models. The Pacific Republic, with its progressive political culture and strong technology industry, would likely pursue strong platform regulation, aggressive content moderation against disinformation and hate speech, data privacy protections, and public media investment — a model roughly analogous to the EU's Digital Services Act but more interventionist. The Gulf Compact, with its nationalist-capitalist ideology and energy industry dominance, would pursue minimal platform regulation, corporate-controlled content governance, weak privacy protections, and strong information sovereignty requirements that prioritize domestic control over international platforms. The Northeast Corridor, with its dense population, international orientation, and mixed political culture, would pursue a European-style balanced model with strong privacy protections, moderate content moderation, and active participation in international information governance frameworks.

These divergent approaches are likely to harden into legal regimes by the mid-2040s because each successor state will have had a decade or more to develop its regulatory infrastructure, because the technology platforms themselves will adapt to each regime (creating different user experiences in each state), and because cross-successor-state information flows will be governed by treaty rather than by a single federal framework. The incompatibility threshold is reached when a platform operating in the Pacific Republic must comply with content rules that would be illegal under the Gulf Compact's free-expression provisions, or when a citizen's data rights in the Northeast Corridor are violated by standards legal in other successor states.

## What Would Falsify This

- All successor states adopt similar information governance frameworks (e.g., a common market agreement for digital services)
- Cross-successor-state coordination bodies prevent fragmentation (e.g., a successor state equivalent of the EU Digital Services Act)
- The number of successor states stabilizes at two or fewer, reducing the condition for "at least three" regimes
- Technology platforms successfully implement uniform global policies that override successor state variation
- A single successor state dominates the others economically or politically, imposing its information governance model

## Confidence Criteria

- **HIGH:** Strong evidence across multiple domains, consistent with established trends
- **MEDIUM:** Plausible with supporting logic, some uncertainty
- **LOW:** Speculative but worth tracking; single line of evidence or contrarian signal

The LOW confidence reflects the significant uncertainty around both the number of successor states and their policy choices. The direction (divergence) is likely — successor states have different political cultures and incentives, and information sovereignty is a natural tool of state-building — but the specific "at least three regimes with legal incompatibility" threshold is speculative. Successor states may coordinate on information governance despite political differences, recognizing the economic value of a unified digital market. The technology landscape may also evolve in unexpected directions — decentralized platforms, AI-mediated content, or global governance frameworks that emerge between now and 2045 could reshape the environment in ways that make successor state regulation less relevant.
