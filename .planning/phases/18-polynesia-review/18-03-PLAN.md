<!-- Distinctive economic models across the 6 entities -->
The Polynesian entities have three distinct economic models:
1. **Diaspora-remittance economies (Cook Is., Niue, Samoa, Tonga):** Remittances from large diaspora populations in NZ, Australia, and HFS are the dominant economic feature. Tourism as secondary revenue. Subsistence agriculture residual.
2. **Post-colonial transition economy (Maohi Nui):** Transition from French budget subsidy dependence to independent revenue. Tourism, pearl farming (black pearls), and fisheries (vast EEZ) as primary sectors. Nuclear reparations as an ongoing revenue stream.
3. **Climate relocation economy (Tuvalu):** EEZ fisheries licensing as primary revenue. Managed relocation funding from Australia and NZ. Remittances from relocated Tuvaluan community. Minimal domestic productive base.

**Cook Is.:** Remittance-and-tourism micro-economy — diaspora-driven and NZ-integrated.

Post-independence (2035-2045), Maohi Nui must replace this with independent revenue. Tourism (Tahiti as the premier South Pacific destination), black pearl farming (Tuamotus — world center, ~$100M+/year at peak), and tuna fisheries licensing (4.8M sq km EEZ, one of the world's largest) are the three pillars. French nuclear reparations provide supplementary revenue.

By 2050 the transition is still underway — tourism has partially recovered, pearl market is stable, but full French transfer replacement is not yet achieved.
- **Key sectors:** Tourism (luxury and eco-tourism), black pearls, tuna fisheries (EEZ licensing and processing), French residual (reparations, aid transition).
- **→ See KML: Maohi Nui**

**Niue:** NZ-subsidized micro-economy — the world's smallest national economy, viable only through NZ budget partnership.
- **Economic structure:** NZ budget support is the economic foundation — NZ provides ~$20M+/year in direct budget support that covers the majority of government services (health, education, infrastructure). Remittances from the ~30K Niuean diaspora in NZ supplement household incomes. Small-scale tourism (ecotourism, diving, whale watching) provides modest local employment. Agriculture (vanilla, taro, honey) for local consumption and limited export.
- **GDP trajectory:** ~$30-40M PPP — essentially flat. The economy is not growth-oriented but viability-oriented. The in-country population is too small to generate significant domestic economic activity. Niue's economic viability is entirely dependent on NZ's continued commitment to the free association arrangement.
- **Key sectors:** NZ budget support, remittances, tourism (small-scale), subsistence agriculture.
- **→ See KML: Niue**

**Samoa:** Remittance-anchored Pacific economy — stable, diaspora-integrated, with tourism and agricultural exports as secondary pillars.
- **Economic structure:** Remittances from the Samoan diaspora (NZ ~150K, Australia ~75K, HFS ~30K) are the largest revenue source — structurally stable and counter-cyclical. Tourism growth toward pre-pandemic levels — Samoan culture, beaches, and ecotourism as attractions. Agricultural exports: nonu juice, coconut products (copra, coconut oil, cream), cocoa, taro. Small manufacturing (garments, auto parts via Yazaki) residual.
- **GDP trajectory:** ~$1-1.2B PPP — modest growth driven by tourism recovery and remittance stability. Samoa's proximity to NZ and Australia, plus the diaspora structure, provides economic resilience that most comparably-sized Pacific states lack.
- **Key sectors:** Remittances, tourism, agriculture (nonu, coconut), fisheries.
- **→ See KML: Samoa**

**Tonga:** Remittance-dependent constitutional monarchy — diaspora flows sustain the economy; tourism and agriculture secondary.
- **Economic structure:** Remittances from the Tongan diaspora (NZ ~60K, Australia ~30K, HFS ~20K) are the largest revenue source — remittance-to-GDP ratio is among the highest in the world. Tourism (whale watching, diving, Vava'u sailing, cultural tourism) is the primary in-country economic activity. Agricultural exports: squash pumpkins (to Japan, Korea), vanilla, root crops. Fisheries (tuna) provide some export revenue.
- **GDP trajectory:** ~$600-700M PPP — stable but slow growth. The 2022 Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai eruption caused a sharp GDP contraction (~20%), illustrating Tonga's extreme vulnerability to natural disasters. Recovery was diaspora-funded.
- **Key sectors:** Remittances, tourism, agriculture (squash, vanilla), fisheries.
- **→ See KML: Tonga**

Relocation funding from Australia (staged relocation agreement) and NZ (Pacific Access Category) provides additional revenue. Remittances from relocated Tuvaluans in Australia and NZ supplement household incomes. No significant agricultural or manufacturing base — the in-country economic structure is minimal and contracting.
- **GDP trajectory:** ~$50-60M PPP — small and declining with in-country population contraction. Per-capita GDP is steady or improving as the population relocates faster than EEZ revenue declines. The economic unit is the diaspora-plus-EEZ complex — Tuvalu's economic sovereignty does not depend on physical habitation.
- **Key sectors:** EEZ fisheries licensing, Australia relocation funding, remittances, Tuvalu Trust Fund (sovereign wealth fund drawdown).
- **→ See KML: Tuvalu**

#### Polynesia

**Cook Is.:** ~17K in-country residents (2050 est.) — one of the world's most extreme diaspora-to-in-country population ratios.
- **Composition:** Cook Islands Māori (78%), Part-Māori (15%), other (7%) — NZ European and Pacific. In-country population is majority Cook Islands Māori; the NZ diaspora (~80K+) is younger and more mixed. Rarotonga concentrates ~75% of the in-country population; the Northern Group atolls (Manihiki, Penrhyn, Pukapuka) are thinly populated.
- **Migration dynamics:** Decades of net migration to NZ (free association = free movement for Cook Islanders) created the diaspora-dominant structure. By 2050, the in-country population has stabilized — return migration of Cook Islanders from NZ driven by lifestyle and sovereignty opportunities. Circular migration (NZ ↔ Cook Islands) is the dominant pattern — not binary settlement.
- **→ See KML: Cook Is.**

**Maohi Nui (French Polynesia):** ~275K residents (2050 est.) — the largest Polynesian population after NZ Māori.
- **Composition:** Polynesian (Mā'ohi) ~78%, European (French descent) ~12%, Chinese descent ~6%, mixed/other ~4%. Tahiti concentrates ~70%+ of the population (Pape'ete urban area). The Tuamotus and Australs are sparsely populated with declining populations as younger generations move to Tahiti or France (pre-independence) or remain in-country post-independence.
- **Migration dynamics:** Pre-independence: net migration of French administrative and military personnel departing as independence approached. Post-independence: reduced emigration to France (Mā'ohi choose to remain in the newly sovereign state). Some return migration of diaspora from France. Internal migration from outer islands to Tahiti continues.
- **→ See KML: Maohi Nui**

**Niue:** ~1,600 in-country residents (2050 est.) — the world's smallest sovereign political community by population.
- **Composition:** Niuean (95%), part-Niuean (3%), other (2%). The in-country population is among the oldest in the Pacific — the out-migration of working-age adults to NZ has left an elderly skew. The Niuean diaspora in NZ (~30K) is younger and more balanced.
- **Migration dynamics:** Peak population was ~5,000 in the 1960s; steady decline to the current micro-population driven by NZ free access (Niueans are NZ citizens). By 2050 the decline has bottomed out — the remaining population consists of those who choose Niue's lifestyle and community over NZ's economic opportunity. The governance challenge is service delivery — health, education, and infrastructure for a population this small and dispersed requires NZ budget support regardless of governance quality.
- **→ See KML: Niue**

**Samoa:** ~225K in-country residents (2050 est.) — stable, diaspora-integrated population with one of the highest diasporas-per-capita ratios globally.
- **Composition:** Samoan (~96%), mixed/other (~4%). Fa'asamoa (the Samoan Way) structures identity regardless of location — Samoan culture maintains cohesion across the diaspora-NZ, diaspora-Australia, diaspora-HFS, and in-country populations. The aiga (extended family) system operates as a transnational governance structure.
- **Migration dynamics:** Net migration roughly stable — out-migration to NZ, Australia, and HFS balanced by return migration and diaspora circulation. The diaspora population (~150K NZ, ~75K Australia, ~30K HFS) is larger than the in-country population, but circular migration keeps the connection active. Remittance flows maintain family ties. Upolu (Apia urban area) concentrates ~75% of the in-country population; Savai'i is rural and aging.
- **→ See KML: Samoa**

**Tonga:** ~100K in-country residents (2050 est.) — diaspora-dominant nationality with high remittance dependency.
- **Composition:** Tongan (~97%), mixed/other (~3%). Tongan identity is unusually cohesive for a Pacific diaspora state — the monarchy, the language (lea faka-Tonga), and the never-colonized status create a national identity that transcends geography. The diaspora in NZ (~60K), Australia (~30K), and HFS (~20K) maintains strong ties.
- **Migration dynamics:** Net out-migration continues — Tonga's population has been roughly stable at ~100K for decades only because births exceed deaths; without emigration, the population would be much higher. Emigration to NZ (Pacific Access Category), Australia, and HFS is the safety valve. The 2022 Hunga eruption briefly reversed migration as aid workers and returning diaspora arrived, but the long-term trend is steady outward flow. Young Tongans leave for education and opportunity; the in-country population ages.
- **→ See KML: Tonga**

**Tuvalu:** ~8K in-country residents (2050 est.) — declining through managed relocation; effective diaspora-dominant nationality in formation.
- **Composition:** Tuvaluan (97%), other Pacific (2%), European/Asian (1%). Culturally homogeneous — the Tuvaluan language, the maneapa (meeting house) system, and the ocean-centered identity structure both in-country and diaspora communities. Funafuti concentrates ~60% of the in-country population; the outer atolls are depopulating fastest.
- **Migration dynamics:** Managed relocation to Australia (staged relocation agreement) and NZ (Pacific Access Category) is the primary demographic driver. In-country population declining from ~11K to ~8K by 2050 as relocation accelerates. The Tuvaluan diaspora in Australia and NZ is growing toward population parity with in-country Tuvaluans. National identity persists across the diaspora — digital governance mechanisms (e-voting, virtual maneapa) maintain political participation. By 2075, the majority of Tuvaluans will be diaspora-based, but Tuvalu continues as a sovereign state.
- **→ See KML: Tuvalu**
