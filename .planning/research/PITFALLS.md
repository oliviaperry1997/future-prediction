# Domain Pitfalls: Long-Range (50-75 Year) Geopolitical Forecasting

**Domain:** Long-range geopolitical futures / world-building
**Researched:** 2026-05-19
**Overall confidence in this document:** HIGH (synthesized from Tetlock's Good Judgment Project research, Dorr's formal fallacies analysis, scenario planning practitioner experience, and post-mortems of major forecasting failures)

---

## Critical Pitfalls

Mistakes that would undermine the entire project's credibility and internal coherence.

### Pitfall 1: The Hedgehog Trap — A Single Big Idea That Organizes Everything

**What goes wrong:** The forecaster adopts one master narrative (e.g., "the US empire must collapse," "socialism is inevitable," "technology will solve everything") and filters all evidence through that lens. Contradictory data is dismissed, ambiguous data is read as supporting the theory.

**Why it happens:** Tetlock's two-decade Good Judgment Project (documented in *Superforecasting*, 2015) demonstrated conclusively that "hedgehogs" — forecasters who organize thinking around one Big Idea — perform significantly worse than "foxes" who draw on multiple frameworks. Hedgehogs' worst performance is on *long-term* forecasts in their *own* area of expertise, because their theory gives them false confidence and they ignore disconfirming evidence.

**Consequences:** The entire forecast becomes a self-fulfilling narrative rather than a genuine exploration of plausible futures. Key uncertainties are collapsed into certainties. The project reads as advocacy or ideology, not analysis.

**Warning signs:**
- You find yourself explaining away events that contradict the central thesis
- Every domain analysis (climate, technology, demographics) independently "points to" the same conclusion
- The "counterargument" section is short or perfunctory
- You feel a sense of satisfaction/clarity rather than discomfort when writing projections

**Prevention strategy:**
- Deliberately write a "counter-scenario" where the central thesis does NOT happen (US adapts, decline reverses, socialism doesn't materialize)
- Use the fox mindset explicitly: for each forecast, ask "what would a Marxist say? A realist? A techno-optimist? A Malthusian?"
- Track your Brier score (or at minimum, assess calibration — see Pitfall 9)
- Include explicit sections in each milestone called "What would falsify this forecast?"

**Phase to address in:** All phases, but especially the initial framing of each milestone's thesis. Dedicate part of Phase 1 (2050 setup) to articulating the project's core assumptions as *falsifiable hypotheses* rather than *narrative premises*.

**Sources:** Tetlock & Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015); Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment* (2005). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 2: The Linear Projection Fallacy

**What goes wrong:** Assuming that current trends continue in a straight line — that the pace of US decline, climate change, technological growth, demographic shifts, etc., will proceed smoothly from present conditions.

**Why it happens:** Human intuition is poorly equipped for exponential or nonlinear change (Dorr, 2017). The "cone of plausibility" expands rapidly with time, but our instinct is to narrow it to a line.

**Consequences for this project:** At 50-75 year horizons, this is the single most dangerous error. A "steady decline" projection for the US that doesn't account for potential crises, pivots, collapses, or resurgences will look naive. A linear climate projection misses tipping points. A linear technology projection misses Moore's-law-style accelerations or plateau.

**Specific manifestations to watch:**
- US military spending as % of GDP declines smoothly → misses sudden strategic shocks
- Birth rates change linearly → misses cultural/catastrophe inflection points
- China's GDP growth steadily converges with US → misses demographic cliff or financial crisis
- The "capitalism to socialism" transition happens in a gradual, orderly way

**Prevention strategy:**
- For every key trend, note the *shape* of change: linear, exponential, S-curve, step-function, cyclical
- Identify trigger thresholds: what would accelerate or reverse each trend?
- Use the futures cone explicitly: probable, plausible, possible, and preferable futures (Voros, 2001)
- Ask: "What would make this trend go nonlinear?"

**Phase to address in:** All domain analyses (climate, economy, technology, demographics). Flag in the initial methodology doc.

**Sources:** Dorr, "Common errors in reasoning about the future: Three informal fallacies," *Technological Forecasting and Social Change* (2017). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 3: The End-of-History Illusion (Presentism)

**What goes wrong:** Projecting today's ideological categories, political structures, and cultural assumptions onto 50-75 year timeframes as if they're permanent. Believing that "socialism" and "capitalism" will still be the defining axis of political economy in 2100.

**Why it happens:** The "End of History" illusion (Gilbert & Quoidbach, 2011) is the tendency to believe that the present moment is the final, mature form of something. Francis Fukuyama's 1989 "End of History" thesis — that liberal democracy had won the ideological battle permanently — is the classic geopolitical example. By 2026, this looks quaint.

**Consequences:** The forecast ages poorly because it's anchored to obsolete categories. The 2050 snapshot that assumes "the US-China rivalry" remains the organizing axis of geopolitics looks silly if a completely different configuration (e.g., regional blocs, AI-governed polities, climate-driven migration states) has emerged.

**Warning signs:**
- You use current political terminology as if it's timeless ("socialism," "capitalism," "democracy," "empire")
- The 2100 snapshot looks like today but with different labels
- You haven't considered entirely new forms of political organization
- The forecast's categories would be recognizable to someone from 2026

**Prevention strategy:**
- Look back 75 years from today (1951): how many of *that* era's categories still apply? (Cold War framing, colonial empires, industrial economy, broadcast media)
- Explicitly list assumptions about category stability
- Consider at least one "radical discontinuity" scenario where the organizing logic of world politics is unrecognizable
- Read historical forecasts from 50-75 years ago to see what they got wrong about categories

**Phase to address in:** Methodology upfront. Each milestone snapshot should have a "categories that might not survive" section.

**Sources:** Gilbert & Quoidbach, "The End of History Illusion" (2011); Fukuyama, *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992); retrospective analyses of forecasts from 1950s-70s. HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 4: Motivated Reasoning / Wishful Thinking (The Desired Future)

**What goes wrong:** The forecast's conclusions align suspiciously well with what the author *wants* to happen. The US collapses (because the author believes empire is unjust). Socialism wins (because the author prefers socialist systems). The transition is relatively peaceful (because the author hopes for a just transition).

**Why it happens:** "Hot" cognition — beliefs shaped by emotional and motivational factors. Burgh & Melo's formal model (2023) shows how wishful thinking systematically distorts probability estimates, especially for desirable vs. undesirable outcomes. Tetlock found that ideology did not predict accuracy (liberals and conservatives were equally bad), but *attachment* to outcomes did.

**Consequences:** The project becomes advocacy in the guise of analysis. It loses credibility with readers who don't share the author's preferences. More importantly, the author misses real risks and alternative pathways because they're unpleasant to consider.

**Warning signs:**
- The "good" outcomes have more detailed, satisfying descriptions than the "bad" ones
- Undesirable but plausible scenarios (e.g., authoritarian eco-socialism, failed state collapse, new imperialism) are hand-waved
- The US collapse narrative is more detailed and emotionally rich than the adaptation/reform scenario
- You notice yourself using passive voice to describe undesired outcomes ("fascism emerges" vs "a fascist movement successfully seizes power")

**Prevention strategy:**
- Write the "nightmare scenario" in equal detail to the preferred scenario
- For each desired outcome, explicitly state: "I want this to happen, which means I should be *more* skeptical of this forecast"
- Use Red Team / devil's advocate as a formal step before finalizing each milestone
- Track whether your forecasts systematically cluster on one side of the probability scale

**Phase to address in:** Every phase. This is a persistent meta-risk. Most relevant in the 2050 and 2075 snapshots where the transition narrative is built.

**Sources:** Burgh & Melo, "Wishful Thinking is Risky Thinking" (2023, SSRN); Tetlock, *Expert Political Judgment* (2005). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 5: The Arrival Fallacy — Treating Snapshots as Static Endpoints

**What goes wrong:** Each milestone snapshot (2050, 2075, 2100) is treated as a stable resting state — "this is what the world looks like at that date" — rather than a dynamic moment in an ongoing process.

**Why it happens:** The snapshot format (KML maps, domain-organized documents) naturally invites endpoint thinking. It's easier to describe a static world than a velocity field.

**Consequences:** The maps look absurdly specific (precise borders at a date) while the transition logic is vague. A border drawn at 2050 implies a permanence that the project's own transition doc might contradict. The 2100 world feels like a "destination" rather than a waypoint.

**Specific manifestations:**
- Borders on KML maps imply stability that may not exist (unrecognized wars, fluid frontiers, contested zones)
- Political regimes described as settled ("socialist federation of X") that would actually be fragile
- No contingency for what *follows* 2050 eroding the 2050 snapshot

**Prevention strategy:**
- Always pair each snapshot with a "dynamics" appendix: what forces are in motion at this moment that will reshape the subsequent period
- Use stippled/dashed borders for uncertain boundaries, solid only where stability is grounded
- Include "what's emerging" and "what's dissolving" sections for every domain
- The transition docs between milestones are *more important* than the snapshots themselves

**Phase to address in:** Individual snapshot creation (each milestone). Also flag in the methodology upfront.

**Sources:** Dorr, "Three Informal Fallacies" (2017); Milojević, "Futures Fallacies" (2021, *Journal of Futures Studies*). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 6: False Precision — Overconfident Detail

**What goes wrong:** The maps and forecasts include detail that cannot be justified by the methodology. A KML polygon showing "Republic of California" in 2050 suggests a level of certainty that is unwarranted at that horizon.

**Why it happens:** Maps naturally demand specificity. The medium (KML, Google Earth) fights ambiguity. And there's a psychological reward to producing concrete details — they feel more "real."

**Consequences:** The project looks amateurish rather than professional. Professional futurists use the futures cone: they distinguish zones of relative confidence (demographics 20 years out) from zones of high uncertainty (political borders 50 years out). Amateur forecasts treat all details as equally knowable.

**Warning signs:**
- Borders at 2100 are drawn with the same confidence as borders at 2030
- Every region of the world gets equal detail regardless of data quality
- The maps include features (new capitals, renamed countries, precise boundaries) with no justification for that level of precision
- There's no uncertainty layer on the maps

**Prevention strategy:**
- Use transparency/opacity on KML polygons to encode confidence: more transparent = lower confidence
- Distinguish between "high confidence" features (climate zones, demographic structures, resource constraints) and "low confidence" features (political borders, leadership, precise alliances)
- For border changes, use buffers/zones of ambiguity rather than sharp lines where appropriate
- Include a legend showing confidence levels for each map feature
- Reference the "cone of plausibility" explicitly: 2050 has a narrower cone than 2100

**Phase to address in:** KML map creation phase and methodology document.

**Sources:** Voros, "A Primer on Futures Studies, Foresight and the Use of Scenarios" (2001); Taylor, "Alternative World Scenarios for Strategic Planning" (U.S. Army War College, 1988). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 7: The Ceteris Paribus Fallacy

**What goes wrong:** Analyzing changes in one domain (e.g., geopolitics) while implicitly holding all others constant (climate, technology, economy, demography, culture) — or analyzing each domain in isolation and then weakly coupling them.

**Why it happens:** It's cognitively easier and produces cleaner narratives. Domain-by-domain analysis (the project's planned structure) invites this fallacy.

**Consequences:** Each domain chapter reads as if its chosen variable is the *only* thing changing. The technology section assumes politics-as-usual. The climate section assumes technology-as-usual. The resulting "2050 world" is a Frankenstein composite of five separate futures that don't fit together.

**Specific to this project:** The project plans six domains (borders/geopolitics, climate, technology, economy, demographics, culture). If each is analyzed independently, the 2050 snapshot will contain internal contradictions — a level of technological transformation that contradicts the political structure, a climate scenario that contradicts the economic assumptions, etc.

**Prevention strategy:**
- Use cross-impact matrices: for each domain projection, explicitly note "if this happens in domain X, what does it imply for domain Y?"
- Build the milestone snapshot iteratively: first draft all domains, then cross-check for contradictions, reconcile, iterate
- Identify the 2-3 "master variables" that most constrain/shaper the others (likely: climate impacts and technology level)
- Include a "synthesis and reconciliation" step that's equal in effort to the individual domain analyses

**Phase to address in:** Milestone snapshot creation methodology.

**Sources:** Dorr, "Three Informal Fallacies" (2017). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 8: Declinism — The Narrative of Collapse Mistaken for Analysis

**What goes wrong:** Applying Paul Kennedy's "imperial overstretch" thesis to the US in a way that conflates *relative decline* with *collapse*. Kennedy himself (1987) was right about long-term patterns of great-power rise and fall, but his timing was famously off (the US "won" the Cold War two years after his book argued the US was overstretched).

**Why it happens:** Collapse narratives are compelling. They provide narrative arc, moral clarity, and dramatic stakes. They're also easier to write than nuanced stories of adaptation, managed decline, or contingent outcomes. And a project predicated on "the collapse of the US empire" has a vested interest in declaring collapse certain.

**Consequences:** The forecast overstates the speed and totality of US decline, misses paths where the US adapts, reforms, or maintains dominance through new means (e.g., AI leadership, energy independence, new alliances). The 2050 and 2075 snapshots become "the US is gone" rather than "the US is different."

**Warning signs:**
- "Decline," "collapse," and "end" are used interchangeably
- The US in 2050 has zero major capabilities rather than reduced/transformed ones
- No plausible reform or adaptation path for the US is described
- The USSR collapse (rapid, total) is used as the model rather than the UK decline (gradual, managed over decades)

**Prevention strategy:**
- Distinguish clearly between absolute decline, relative decline, collapse, and transformation
- Look at historical precedents for great-power transitions: UK-to-US (peaceful, decades), USSR-to-Russia (rapid, chaotic), Rome's fall (centuries, contested)
- Write at least one "the US adapts" scenario in detail — it doesn't have to be the preferred scenario, but it must be plausible
- Track military spending, technological edge, alliance depth, soft power, economic dynamism separately rather than as "US power" aggregated

**Phase to address in:** Initial framing and thesis development. The "central thesis" (US collapse) must be handled with care to avoid becoming a declinist narrative.

**Sources:** Kennedy, *The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers* (1987); Cox, "For over 30 years, Paul Kennedy's The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers has been the backdrop..." (LSE, 2023); Brooks & Wohlforth, "The Rise and Fall of the Great Powers in the 21st Century" (International Security). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 9: No Calibration Tracking — The Amateur vs Professional Divide

**What goes wrong:** Making forecasts without systematically tracking accuracy. The author has no idea whether their forecasts are well-calibrated (events predicted at 70% happen 70% of the time).

**Why it happens:** Calibration tracking requires:
1. Explicit numerical probability estimates for specific events
2. A record of those estimates
3. A method to check outcomes
4. The discipline to keep doing it

Most amateurs skip this entirely. Without it, you can't distinguish a genuinely skilled forecaster from a confident one.

**The amateur vs professional gap:** Tetlock found that the most confident experts (the ones on TV) were no better than dart-throwing chimps. Superforecasters — who *did* beat the chimps — were humble, revised frequently, and tracked their scores. The key professional markers are: explicit probabilities, frequent updating, honest error tracking. Their absence is the single best indicator of amateur work.

**Consequences for this project:** Without calibration, the project can't improve. It will express the same level of confidence for robust predictions and wild guesses. More than that, it will *look* amateurish to anyone familiar with the forecasting literature.

**Prevention strategy:**
- Attach explicit confidence levels (HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW) to every major forecast claim
- For specific predictions within the project, assign numerical probabilities where possible
- Create a "prediction register" — a spreadsheet of specific, falsifiable claims with dates and probabilities
- After each milestone, review the previous milestone's predictions against actual events (even partial outcomes)
- Reference Brier scores and calibration as concepts, even if you can't compute them perfectly for long-range forecasts

**Phase to address in:** Methodology phase upfront. Maintain the prediction register as a living artifact.

**Sources:** Tetlock & Gardner, *Superforecasting* (2015); Tetlock, Mellers et al., "Forecasting Tournaments" (2014, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*). HIGH confidence.

### Pitfall 10: The "Clocklike" vs "Cloudlike" Confusion

**What goes wrong:** Treating "cloudlike" domains (unpredictable, emergent, chaotic) as if they were "clocklike" (deterministic, predictable with enough data).

**Why it happens:** Tetlock distinguishes clocklike questions (predictable with simple rules — e.g., "who wins the World Series?" months before is pointless, but "will the polar ice cap shrink?" is more tractable) from cloudlike ones (inherently unpredictable — e.g., oil prices, currency markets, specific political outcomes at 50-year horizons). Amateurs apply clocklike methods to cloudlike questions.

**Consequences:** The forecast makes specific predictions about inherently unpredictable things (exact borders in 2075, specific alliances in 2100, etc.) while being vague about things that *are* somewhat predictable at 50-year horizons (climate zones, demographic structure, resource constraints).

**Prevention strategy:**
- Map each domain on a clocklike→cloudlike spectrum
- Spend proportionally more analytical effort on cloudlike domains if they're important (i.e., do scenario work, not point predictions)
- For cloudlike questions, describe ranges, families of outcomes, and signposts, not point predictions
- For clocklike questions (demographics, climate physics), invest in quantitative baselines
- Accept that at 50-75 years, almost all political questions are cloudlike

**Phase to address in:** Methodology upfront and each domain analysis.

**Sources:** Tetlock, *Superforecasting* (2015), specifically the triage concept. HIGH confidence.

## Moderate Pitfalls

### Pitfall 11: Technological Determinism vs. Blindness

**What goes wrong:** Either technology drives everything (ignoring politics, culture, contingency) or technology is treated as a black box ("it'll work out"). The project's technology domain section either overpowers all others or is disconnected from them.

**Prevention:** Recognize technology as one driver among many. Watch for implicit assumptions that AI solves everything or nothing. Check for coherence between the technology scenario and the political/economic scenarios — they must be compatible.

**Phase:** Technology domain analysis.

**Source:** Dorr (2017), citing Smith & Marx (1994), Bijker (2007). HIGH confidence.

---

### Pitfall 12: Overweighting Near-Term, Underweighting Far-Term

**What goes wrong:** The 2050 snapshot gets much more detailed attention and care than 2075 and 2100. The project "fades out" as the horizon extends, with increasingly vague descriptions.

**Prevention:** Explicitly grade detail by horizon — but don't let 2100 become an afterthought. The strategic value of the project is in the *full arc* to 2100. Consider starting with 2100 and working backward, or at minimum giving all three snapshots equal structural treatment.

**Phase:** Milestone ordering and effort allocation.

---

### Pitfall 13: Ignoring Second-Order Effects

**What goes wrong:** The forecast accounts for first-order changes (climate gets warmer → agriculture shifts) but ignores second- and third-order effects (agriculture shifts → land values change → new political power centers emerge → borders shift → conflict → refugee flows → political realignment).

**Prevention:** Use systems thinking / causal loop diagrams for the most important chains. For each significant change, ask "and then what happens?" three times.

**Phase:** All domain analyses.

---

### Pitfall 14: Assuming Rationality in Key Actors

**What goes wrong:** Projections assume that states, corporations, and other actors behave rationally — optimizing power, profit, or survival. History suggests otherwise: cognitive biases distort decision-making at the institutional level too.

**Prevention:** Explicitly consider how misperception, groupthink, and organizational dysfunction could drive outcomes. The "rational actor" model is a useful baseline but should never be the only one.

**Phase:** Geopolitics/borders domain.

**Source:** Snyder, *Myths of Empire* (1991); Kahneman & Renshon, "Hawkish Biases" (2007). HIGH confidence.

## Minor Pitfalls

### Minor Pitfall: Assuming Political Borders Are the Primary Unit

Borders/geopolitics is only one of six domains. In a world of powerful corporations, transnational movements, networked governance, and AI systems, states may not be the most important actors by 2075. Balance the border-centric view with other forms of power.

### Minor Pitfall: The "Last War" Problem

Projecting the future based on the last major geopolitical shift (Cold War end, 9/11, COVID, Ukraine). The next 50 years will be shaped by forces we haven't named yet. Leave room for the genuinely novel.

### Minor Pitfall: One-Person Blindness

A solo project has no peer review, no devil's advocate, no diversity of perspective. This is inherent to the project's constraints, so compensate with structured self-challenge: write counter-scenarios, use explicit debiasing checklists, seek occasional external review where possible.

**Phase:** Project setup. Mitigation built into methodology.

## Summary: Professional vs. Amateur Forecasting — A Diagnostic

| Attribute | Amateur | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| Certainty | High, stated as fact | Probabilistic, confidence graded |
| Big idea | One master narrative drives everything | Multiple frameworks held simultaneously |
| Detail | Uniform precision regardless of horizon | Confidence gradients; less detail for longer horizons |
| Error handling | Mistakes ignored or rationalized | Errors tracked, calibration measured |
| Scope | Only confirms the thesis | Counter-scenarios written in equal detail |
| Updates | Fixed once written | Revised as new evidence emerges |
| Map precision | Sharp borders everywhere | Explicit uncertainty encoding |
| Time horizon | All treated the same | Cone of plausibility acknowledged |

---

## The Most Important Thing for This Project

The project's central thesis — US collapse and transition to socialism — is itself the biggest risk. It's a powerful narrative that could easily become a hedgehog trap. **The project's credibility depends not on being "right" but on being rigorous — showing the work, the uncertainties, the alternatives, and the honest calibration between what's known and what's guessed.**

The single best practice: for every forecast you're confident about, write the version where you're wrong in detail. If you find yourself unable to do that convincingly, you're probably hedgehogging.
