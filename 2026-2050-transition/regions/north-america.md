# North America (ex-US): 2026-2049

**Domain:** `= this.domain`
**Milestone:** `= this.milestone`
**Status:** `= this.status`

## Overview

The US dissolution is the dominant force reshaping North America. Canada and Mexico — economically integrated into the US market through USMCA, militarily dependent on US security guarantees, and politically accustomed to a stable northern neighbor — must fundamentally reconfigure their relationships, economies, and internal governance. The Caribbean faces the collapse of its primary trading partner and tourist market while confronting accelerating climate impacts. The Arctic becomes a contested strategic zone as the US Coast Guard and military presence fragment, leaving Canada alone to manage the Northwest Passage; Greenland navigates the tension between independence ambitions and economic dependence on Denmark.

## Key Actors

### 1. Canada (Holds Together, Regionally Reconstituted)
- **Orientation:** Survives as a federation through deep regional reconfiguration (MEDIUM confidence)
- **Core territory question:** Canada holds together. The "external container" thesis — that US collapse would trigger Canadian fragmentation — overestimated the centrifugal forces. Canadian national identity, institutional cohesion, and the sheer complexity of dismantling a federation prove stronger than the fragmentation model assumed.
- **Economic base:** Resource extraction (oil sands — Alberta, $100B+ in cumulative stranded asset risk), manufacturing (Ontario/Quebec, auto industry heavily integrated with US — eastward pivot to NEC/GLPR trade), agriculture (Prairies), forestry/hydro (BC).
- **Regional reorientation:** Western Canada reorients toward Asia-Pacific trade through BC ports. Quebec deepens European economic ties while remaining in the federation. Atlantic Canada pivots toward NEC and Europe. BC develops deep Cascadian economic integration with the PPR — functional cross-border trade and cultural affinity without political separation from Canada.
- **Quebec sovereignty:** The sovereignty movement gains ground as federal weakness deepens. A third referendum (~2036-2040) is plausible but results in either a narrow federalist victory or a negotiated settlement granting Quebec enhanced autonomy (asymmetric federalism) rather than full exit. The costs of independence — currency separation, debt division, border renegotiation with a fragmenting US — prove sufficiently deterrent.
- **Alberta:** Western alienation intensifies and an Alberta separation movement surges in the early 2030s as oil sands become stranded assets. However, the economic reality of an independent Alberta — a landlocked petro-state with no trade corridor and a collapsing resource — prevents actual secession. Instead, Alberta extracts major fiscal and constitutional concessions within Canada.
- **BC:** Deepens economic and cultural integration with the PPR's Cascadian region (Washington/Oregon). This is functional and practical — shared grids, transportation corridors, cultural affinity — not political. BC remains firmly Canadian. The strongest Cascadian pull is limited to the Vancouver-Seattle-Portland corridor.
- **Trajectory:** Canada emerges from the transition battered, poorer, and regionally reconfigured — but intact. By 2049, Canada is a decentralized federation: Quebec holds enhanced autonomy, Alberta wields outsized influence through resource leverage, BC functions as Canada's Pacific bridge, and Atlantic Canada orients eastward. The Canadian state is weaker and looser than its pre-2026 form, but it survives.
- **Links to US collapse:** USMCA is dead by ~2034. Canadian dollar depreciates alongside the US dollar. Canada's military (CAF) loses US logistics/intelligence support. The 5,500-mile undefended border becomes a complex multi-jurisdictional frontier as US successor states control different sections. Canada negotiates separate trade and security arrangements with the NEC, PPR, and Gulf Compact.

### 2. Mexico (Unified with Aztlán)
- **Orientation:** Survives unified; northern border reconfigured (HIGH confidence)
- **Core territory:** Mexico's northern states remain fully Mexican. Baja California stays firmly with Mexico — no PPR absorption occurs. The maquiladora corridor (Nuevo León, Tamaulipas, Chihuahua, Sonora, Baja California) reorients toward domestic and Latin American supply chains.
- **Economic base:** Manufacturing (maquiladora corridor — heavily integrated with former US supply chains, loses 40-60% of US demand), remittances ($60B+ annually — critical for millions of households, disrupted by US fragmentation causing humanitarian crisis ~2034-2036), energy (oil — Pemex declining, renewables potential), agriculture (produce exports, water-stressed north).
- **Internal dynamics:** Cartel violence is exacerbated by the collapse of US federal law enforcement cooperation and the opening of the northern border into multiple jurisdictions with varying capacities. The cartel economy partially fills the gap left by remittance disruption.
- **Aztlán relationship:** The Chicano Socialist Republic's Aztlán nationalism has significant soft-power appeal in northern Mexico, particularly among communities with deep historical ties to Chicano identity. However, the CSR is smaller, weaker, and less industrialized than Mexico. Rather than the CSR pulling northern Mexico away, the reverse occurs over time: as its revolutionary energy matures (~2039-2045), it increasingly integrates into Mexico's economic and political orbit. Mexico — larger, more established, with a stronger manufacturing base — absorbs the CSR's ideological energy. By 2049, the CSR (renamed Aztlán) is functionally a special economic zone within Mexico's sphere: ideologically aligned (Mexico under continued left governance trends toward market socialism), economically integrated, and no longer a separatist project. The Aztlán dream fades into practical cross-border cooperation.
- **Trajectory:** Mexico survives as a unified state through the transition. Its northern border becomes a complex multi-jurisdictional frontier. Trade with the former US drops 40-60%, forcing reorientation toward domestic markets and Latin American trade partners. Mexico deepens ties with Brazil and BRICS+. By 2049, Mexico is a middle-income transitional economy — still market-oriented but with growing state intervention, trending toward the Brazilian model of developmental socialism rather than the collapsing US neoliberal model.
- **Links to US collapse:** Remittance disruption causes a humanitarian crisis in ~2034-2036 (millions of families lose their primary income). Mexico becomes a key diplomatic bridge between the Aztlán and the broader Latin American left. Mexico's energy security improves as US demand for its oil ceases and domestic consumption takes priority.

### 3. Caribbean (Humanitarian Crisis, Post-US Reconfiguration)
- **Orientation:** Deep economic collapse; fragmented recovery paths (MEDIUM confidence)
- **Core territory:** Island states — Cuba, Dominican Republic, Puerto Rico, Haiti, Jamaica, Bahamas, Cayman, BVI, Eastern Caribbean microstates.
- **Economic base:** Tourism (20-40% of GDP for most islands — collapses as US successor states have no disposable income for leisure travel). Offshore finance (Cayman, BVI, Bahamas — dollar-denominated finance shrinks with the dollar's reserve status). Remittances (Haiti >35% GDP, Jamaica ~20%, DR ~10% — heavily dependent on US-based diaspora, disrupted by US fragmentation). Free-zone manufacturing (DR — $7.9B, 73% to US market — vulnerable to US demand collapse).
- **Cuba:** US collapse removes the embargo enforcement mechanism. Cuba normalizes relations with CSR and NEC, which take the lead in re-establishing trade and diplomatic ties. Chinese and Russian investment accelerates — Cuba becomes a regional hub for BRICS+ engagement in the Caribbean. However, Venezuela's oil subsidies (already ended by 2026) are not replaced, and Cuba faces its own transition challenges. Trajectory: from isolated socialist outpost to regional hub, but constrained by infrastructure decay and the loss of preferential oil.
- **Puerto Rico:** US federal collapse leaves it in legal limbo — neither a state nor independent, US citizenship rendered meaningless. The debt burden becomes moot as the creditor (US Treasury) disappears. Likely outcome: independence by default (~2035-2040), followed by negotiations for economic association with NEC and CSR.
- **Haiti:** Catastrophic collapse. Remittances (>35% GDP) disappear. International aid fragments. Mass migration to Dominican Republic and Bahamas intensifies. Haiti becomes the Caribbean's worst humanitarian disaster of the transition period.
- **Dominican Republic:** Free-zone manufacturing (73% to US market) collapses as US demand disintegrates. Tourism drops sharply. Remittances shrink. The DR pivots toward Latin American and European markets but faces a severe recession through the 2030s.
- **Offshore financial centers (Cayman, BVI, Bahamas):** Dollar-denominated finance shrinks as the dollar loses reserve status and US regulatory oversight fragments. These centers decline in global significance and reorient toward euro, yuan, and cryptocurrency-based finance.
- **Links to US collapse:** Every island economy contracts 30-60%. The Caribbean becomes a humanitarian crisis zone in the 2030s, with significant migration pressure on Florida (itself fragmented), the Aztlán, and Mexico. Recovery depends on intra-regional trade, BRICS+ investment, and post-tourism economic models (Cuba's healthcare exports, Dominican manufacturing for Latin American markets, digital services).

### 4. Greenland/Arctic (Indigenous Sovereignty, Strategic Reconfiguration)
- **Orientation:** Greenland independence deferred; Arctic governance pluralized (MEDIUM confidence)
- **Core territory:** Greenland (semi-independent within Denmark), Canadian Arctic Archipelago, Alaska (Tlingit Aaní, indigenous territories, Alaska).
- **Greenland independence calculus:** Denmark's annual block grant has declined from 30% of GDP (2003) to ~18.7% (2023), reducing the financial dependency that constrains independence. However, fisheries revenue (~$500M/year) covers government operations but not the full welfare state. Rare earths (Kvanefjeld deposit — one of the world's largest) and uranium offer long-term resource wealth, but mining infrastructure is absent and domestic opposition is significant. Most likely outcome: independence declared in principle (~2038-2042) but full sovereignty phased over a decade as Greenland negotiates continued Danish subsidies and EU partnership. Greenland positions itself as an Arctic indigenous nation with resource sovereignty, not an abruptly independent microstate.
- **US strategic withdrawal:** Pituffik Space Base (formerly Thule) loses strategic value as the US dissolves. Denmark, the EU, and a Nordic-led Arctic security framework fill the gap.
- **Northwest Passage:** Canada loses US naval/coast guard support for sovereignty enforcement. The passage becomes effectively international waters as Canadian capacity to patrol collapses. Russia, China, and the EU vie for transit rights and influence. Canada asserts sovereignty diplomatically but cannot enforce it militarily.
- **Indigenous Arctic governance:** Inuit Nunangat (the Inuit homeland spanning Canada's Arctic, Greenland, and Alaska) becomes a transnational political space. Inuit self-government agreements in Canada (Nunavut, Nunavik, Nunatsiavut) evolve toward de facto sovereignty as Canadian federal authority weakens but does not collapse. The Arctic Council becomes the primary governance forum, with enhanced indigenous permanent participant status.
- **Alaska tripartite division:** Alaska's three regions diverge sharply. The panhandle (Southeast Alaska) becomes the Tlingit Aaní — an independent indigenous confederated socialist republic, PPR-friendly but not part of the PPR, and holding no connection to Canada. Interior and North Slope form an indigenous territorial mosaic with resource control but limited sovereignty. Anchorage and the railbelt remain a reactionary US-loyalist rump, degrading rapidly as federal support vanishes and the oil economy collapses.
- **Links to US collapse:** Arctic security is completely reconfigured. US Coast Guard (icebreaker fleet, Arctic patrol) fragments. NORAD loses US funding and Canadian capacity. The Arctic shifts from a US-dominated security space to a pluralized governance zone where indigenous nations, Nordic states, and a reduced-capacity Canada manage the region cooperatively.

## Cross-Regional Dynamics

- **The PPR pull (narrowed):** Cascadian economic integration affects coastal BC only — the Vancouver-Seattle-Portland corridor — and remains functional and cultural, not political. BC stays Canadian. Baja California stays Mexican. The Alaska panhandle (Tlingit Aaní) is PPR-friendly but independent. No formal PPR expansion beyond former US borders.
- **Aztlán-Mexico convergence:** Rather than CSR pulling northern Mexico away, the reverse occurs: Mexico absorbs CSR's ideological energy as the revolutionary state matures and seeks stability. By the late 2040s, the Aztlán is functionally integrated into Mexico's sphere — a semi-autonomous socialist zone that enriches Mexico's left-governance model rather than challenging its territorial integrity.
- **Caribbean dependency collapse:** The entire region's economy is structured around US tourism, finance, and remittances. All three collapse simultaneously, creating a humanitarian crisis that US successor states (themselves in transition) have limited capacity to address. Recovery depends on BRICS+ investment and post-tourism economic models.
- **Arctic pluralization:** The Arctic becomes a zone of multiple indigenous-governed entities (Greenland, Nunavut, Tlingit Aaní, Alaska interior territories) and reduced great-power competition. Russia and China deal with a fragmented set of Arctic actors rather than a unified US-Canada-Denmark front.
- **Hawaii (Pacific cross-reference):** Hawaii undergoes a distinct arc — short-lived US naval dictatorship (~2030-2033) → popular sovereignty revolution → independent Hawaiian Free State with PPR alliance. Full treatment in the Oceania regional analysis.

## Key Uncertainties

- **Canadian cohesion:** Whether Canadian national identity and institutional resilience are strong enough to withstand the economic shock of US dissolution without significant fragmentation. The central tension: Quebec's sovereignty movement and Western alienation versus the economic and political costs of dismantling the federation. **(MEDIUM)**
- **CSR absorption trajectory:** Whether the Aztlán integrates into Mexico peacefully or whether friction over governance models, resource distribution, and Aztlán nationalism generates conflict. The risk: CSR revolutionary maximalists reject absorption and the relationship becomes hostile. **(MEDIUM)**
- **Caribbean recovery path:** Whether the Caribbean can rebuild around intra-regional trade, BRICS+ investment, and post-tourism economic models, or whether the 2030s become a lost decade. Cuba's transition is the key variable — success demonstrates a replicable model. **(LOW)**
- **Greenland independence timeline:** Whether Greenland achieves full independence by 2049 or remains in phased sovereignty within the Danish realm. The economic viability question — fisheries alone cannot fund a welfare state — suggests phased independence is more likely. **(MEDIUM)**
- **Alaska tripartite stability:** Whether the Tlingit Aaní (indigenous socialist republic), interior indigenous territories, and Alaska coexist peacefully or whether resource competition and ideological conflict generate violence. **(MEDIUM-HIGH)**

## Links to US Successor State System

- The PPR's Cascadian economic integration affects coastal BC — functional cross-border cooperation without political absorption
- Aztlán's ideological energy is absorbed by Mexico over time — the Aztlán project evolves from separatist to integrative
- The NEC engages with eastern Canada (Quebec, Ontario, Atlantic provinces) and the Caribbean as natural Atlantic trade partners
- The Gulf Compact's energy orientation gives it interest in Mexican oil and Caribbean refining — though its economic decline limits reach
- The Arctic becomes a zone of indigenous sovereign entities (Greenland, Nunavut, Tlingit Aaní) rather than a US-dominated security space
- Tlingit Aaní (Alaska panhandle) is an independent indigenous confederated socialist republic — PPR-friendly, not PPR-controlled
