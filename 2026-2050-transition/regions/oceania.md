# Oceania: 2026-2049

**Domain:** `= this.domain`
**Milestone:** `= this.milestone`
**Status:** `= this.status`

## Overview

Oceania in the 2026-2049 period is shaped by three dynamics: (1) the US collapse removes the security architecture (ANZUS, AUKUS, COFA) that structured the region since WWII, (2) Pacific Island states face existential climate threats while their geostrategic importance grows, and (3) the region's economic orientation pivots definitively to Asia. Oceania is a region of extremes — the world's largest stable landmass (Australia) and the world's most vulnerable microstates (Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands) exist in the same geopolitical space. And five US Pacific territories (Guam, American Samoa, Northern Mariana Islands plus minor outlying islands) become constitutional orphans — their federal administrator has collapsed, ~30% of Guam's economy disappears overnight, and their major military bases become contested assets in a power vacuum.

**The US coercion phase in Oceania (2026-2032)** was real but operated differently than in Asia. The US did not demand military contributions from Oceania in the same way it did from Japan or Korea — the region was a security periphery, not a front line. But the US did structure the region through a web of dependencies: ANZUS bound Australia and NZ to US strategic priorities; AUKUS locked Australia into a trillion-dollar bet on US technology transfer; the Compacts of Free Association (COFA) gave the US exclusive military access to Micronesia, Marshall Islands, and Palau in exchange for aid; and US basing (Pine Gap, Darwin marines, Guam, Wake) made Australia and US territories forward operating bases for US power projection. The US collapse removes all of these — ANZUS is dead without the US, AUKUS is unviable without US technology, COFA agreements become void, US bases on Australian territory become orphaned assets, and the US territories themselves lose their federal administrator.

**The post-collapse spring effect** is proportional to prior distortion. Australia was pushed furthest from its natural equilibrium (forced into anti-China posture, AUKUS dependency, hosting US forces) and faces the most dramatic reorientation. Pacific Island states were held in US-aligned dependency through COFA aid and diplomatic pressure — their spring effect is toward genuine non-alignment and "Blue Pacific" regionalism. New Zealand was barely pushed (nuclear-free since 1984, already independent) and barely needs to adjust.

**Oceania's revolutionary/reactionary binary:** In Oceania, **revolutionary** means independent foreign policy, Pacific regionalism, climate justice leadership, BRICS+ engagement, and post-colonial sovereignty. **Reactionary** means US-client dependency, resource extraction without diversification, strategic alignment with declining US, and resistance to Pacific regional integration.

## Key Actors

### 1. Australia — Bifurcation Point

- **Orientation:** Bifurcation — caught between revolutionary multipolar engagement and reactionary US-client inertia (MEDIUM confidence)
- **Population:** ~27M in 2026, growing to ~32M by 2049. Immigration-dependent. Declining but managed demographics.
- **Economic base:** Resources (iron ore $130B+ in exports, coal $70B+ declining, LNG $60B+, lithium, rare earths, copper, gold — critical for green energy transition), services (education, finance, tourism), agriculture (wheat, beef, wool, wine). Australia's economy is resource-export-driven — its prosperity depends on Chinese and Asian industrial demand. China is Australia's largest trading partner by a wide margin (~30% of total trade).
- **The AUKUS trap:** The 2021 AUKUS deal committed Australia to buying 3-5 Virginia-class nuclear submarines from the US (2030s) plus 5 jointly designed SSN-AUKUS boats (2040s). Canberra burned its French submarine deal to do so. By 2026, AUKUS is in deep trouble: the Pentagon launched a formal review in mid-2025, US shipyards can only produce ~1.2 submarines/year against a 2.33 target, Congress questions diverting scarce boats to Australia, and the US has explicit opt-out rights. Australia has already paid $3B into the program but faces a credible scenario where it gets neither US nuclear subs nor the French conventional ones it rejected. The AUKUS review under Trump's "America First" lens reframed the partnership as a transaction rather than an alliance — the US will prioritize its own fleet. AUKUS without the US is dead.
- **Strategic pivot underway:** Australia is already hedging. It contracted Mitsubishi Heavy Industries for 11 next-generation frigates (April 2026). It maintains dialog about BRICS+ observer status (Kazan 2026 summit), NDB non-dollar trade settlement, and expanded trade with India and ASEAN. The US-China "strategic stability" framework (May 2026 Beijing summit) deprioritizes Cold War containment, leaving Australia's anti-China posture stranded. Australia's strategic discourse is shifting from "how to stay aligned with the US" to "how to navigate a world where the US is no longer the dominant Pacific power."
- **The two paths:** The revolutionary path — pragmatic multipolar engagement (BRICS+ observer, Asia-oriented trade diversification, independent defense without AUKUS dependency, Pacific partnership rather than Pacific dominance). The reactionary path — clinging to US-client model (continued AUKUS payments for submarines that never arrive, hosting orphaned US bases, confronting China without US backing, strategic isolation). Australia's trajectory depends on whether the post-US political class can break from 80 years of alliance dependency.
- **Climate vulnerability:** Australia is the most climate-vulnerable developed economy — extreme heat (50°C+ days becoming routine), bushfire seasons lengthening, coral reef collapse (Great Barrier Reef), water scarcity. Climate adaptation is Australia's largest domestic policy challenge.
- **Links to US collapse:** The loss of the US alliance is Australia's most significant strategic shock since WWII. Intelligence sharing (Five Eyes) continues in reduced form. AUKUS is unviable. US bases on Australian territory (Pine Gap, Darwin) lose their strategic rationale. Australia must build an independent posture for the first time since 1942.

### 2. New Zealand — Revolutionary (Small-State Progressive)

- **Orientation:** Revolutionary default — independent foreign policy, nuclear-free, Pacific-focused, progressive governance (HIGH confidence)
- **Population:** ~5.2M in 2026, growing to ~6M by 2049. Immigration from Pacific Islands and Asia increases.
- **Economic base:** Agriculture (dairy $20B+ exports, meat, wool, wine, kiwifruit), tourism (declining with global recession), technology (modest — film, software, agritech). Diversification from agricultural dependency is slow.
- **Revolutionary posture:** New Zealand has been on the revolutionary path since 1984 — nuclear-free policy, suspension from ANZUS by the US, independent foreign policy on Pacific issues, progressive domestic governance (indigenous reconciliation, environmental leadership). The US collapse changes nothing for New Zealand's strategic posture — it was already outside the US orbit. New Zealand is the clearest example in Oceania of a revolutionary small state that chose the correct path decades before the US collapse validated it.
- **The PPR alignment:** NZ has natural ideological alignment with the PPR (progressive environmental policies, indigenous reconciliation, anti-nuclear stance, Pacific Island solidarity). The PPR is NZ's most natural US successor state partner. Trade, climate cooperation, and Pacific diplomacy deepen through the transition.
- **Climate refuge:** NZ's temperate climate, freshwater abundance, stable governance, and low population density make it a potential climate refuge destination. Migration from Pacific Islands increases — NZ expands its Pacific Access Category visa but maintains controlled immigration. By 2049, NZ's population includes a significantly larger Pacific Islander proportion.
- **Trajectory:** NZ continues its comfortable, stable, small-country trajectory — largely unaffected by the global convulsions around it, intentionally peripheral. Its revolutionary posture is not activist or expansionist — it is a quiet model of small-state progressive governance that works.
- **Links to US collapse:** Minimal — NZ was already the most independent ANZUS member. The Five Eyes intelligence relationship continues but is less valuable without US signals intelligence dominance.

### 3. Pacific Islands — Revolutionary (Institutional) / Existential

- **Orientation:** Revolutionary institutional regionalism + existential climate survival — "Blue Pacific" identity as integration framework (MEDIUM confidence)
- **Population:** ~12M total. PNG is the largest (~10M); atoll states are among the world's smallest (Tuvalu ~12K, Nauru ~10K).
- **The integration feedback loop — "Blue Pacific" regionalism:** The Pacific Islands Forum (PIF) 2050 Strategy for the Blue Pacific Continent is Oceania's integration vehicle. Not as formal as the EAF but directionally identical — moving from regional cooperation to "deepened regionalism" (2025 PIF communique). Key institutional achievements: Pacific Resilience Facility (climate adaptation financing), Pacific Islands Forum Fisheries Agency (tuna management — $5B+/year industry), political Talanoa on deepened regional architecture, and the "Blue Pacific" identity as a collective geopolitical brand. The feedback loop: regional cooperation delivers tangible benefits (climate finance, fisheries revenue, diplomatic weight) → institutional deepening attracts resources and respect → member states invest more in regional frameworks → deeper integration. Small states that would be powerless individually gain collective leverage.
- **Climate — the existential driver:** Sea level rise (20-30cm by 2049) threatens the physical existence of low-lying atoll states. Saltwater intrusion destroys freshwater lenses and cropland. Storm surges intensify. The "climate genocide" framing — these states did nothing to cause climate change but face its most extreme consequences — is a powerful moral argument in international forums. By 2049, the first climate-driven state relocations are underway (Tuvalu has a gradual relocation deal with Australia; Kiribati has purchased land in Fiji). Their legal sovereignty and 200nm EEZ maritime zones may persist even after physical territory is compromised — a revolutionary legal innovation in international law.
- **Geostrategic positioning:** The US collapse removes the dominant Pacific security power. China expands its Pacific presence (Solomon Islands security agreement 2022 as the model; policing, infrastructure, diplomacy). Australia and NZ try to maintain traditional leadership but face Chinese competition. The PIF navigates between Chinese and Western interests, maintaining the "Pacific way" of consensus. Pacific states are not choosing sides — they are leveraging geostrategic competition to extract maximum commitments from all parties. This is the revolutionary strategy for microstates: turn great-power competition into a source of leverage rather than a burden.
- **Classification by state:**
  - **Fiji** — revolutionary anchor. Largest Melanesian economy, natural Pacific hub. BRICS+ engagement, independent foreign policy. Hosts UN, diplomatic missions. The most strategically capable Pacific state.
  - **Papua New Guinea** — bifurcation point. Resource wealth (LNG, gold, copper) but governance failure (corruption, law and order, infrastructure). Strategically positioned (land border with Indonesia, astride key shipping lanes). Chinese investment competes with Australian security concern. PNG's trajectory depends on whether it can manage resource wealth and governance — revolutionary potential through BRICS+ commodity strategy, reactionary trap through resource curse. Likely reactionary by default (governance too weak for revolutionary breakthrough).
  - **Solomon Islands** — revolutionary (pragmatic). 2022 security agreement with China broke the US-Australia monopoly. Now has options. Small, poor, but strategically positioned and playing the game effectively.
  - **Atoll states (Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands)** — revolutionary (climate justice). Existential threat forces institutional innovation. Their contribution to the revolutionary framework is legal and moral — they are testing whether sovereignty can survive territory loss.
  - **Micronesia, Palau, Marshall Islands** — post-COFA reorientation. COFA agreements (giving US exclusive military access in exchange for aid) are void without the US. These states must negotiate new arrangements with China, Australia, or regional frameworks. The transition is destabilizing — they lose their primary aid source and security guarantor simultaneously.
  - **Bougainville** — potential revolutionary (independence). Autonomous region of PNG with a deferred independence referendum. If the US collapse demonstrates that small-state sovereignty is viable — and if PNG governance continues to fail — Bougainville independence by ~2038-2042 becomes likely. The Panguna copper mine (closed since 1989) could provide an economic base with Chinese investment.
- **Links to US collapse:** The US was the dominant Pacific security power. COFA agreements voided. US territories (Guam, American Samoa) become US successor state responsibilities — Guam likely aligns with the PPR or HFS. The power vacuum is filled by China, Australia, and regional actors.

### 4. French Pacific — Revolutionary (Decolonization)

- **Orientation:** Decolonization trajectories accelerating post-metropolitan-France-reorientation (LOW confidence)
- **New Caledonia:** Three independence referendums (2018-2021) rejected independence each time, but the post-2026 calculus changes. France, focused on European security and deindustrialization, has less capacity and willingness to maintain expensive Pacific territories. New Caledonia gains expanded autonomy or full independence by the late 2030s-2040s. Nickel reserves (critical for EV batteries) provide economic base.
- **French Polynesia (Tahiti):** Active independence movement. French nuclear testing legacy (193 tests 1966-1996) remains unresolved. France's Pacific military presence (French Forces in Polynesia) is expensive and strategically diminishing. Independence trajectory follows New Caledonia's, possibly faster.
- **Links to US collapse:** Indirect — French Pacific decolonization is driven by metropolitan France's reorientation and the demonstration effect of US successor states and Pacific independence movements.

### 5. US Pacific Territories — Orphaned Post-US (Guam, American Samoa, Northern Mariana Islands)

- **Orientation:** Orphaned territories of a collapsed state — legal status undefined, military assets contested, populations seeking self-determination (LOW confidence)
- **Population:** ~270K total — Guam (~170K), American Samoa (~50K), CNMI (~50K). Plus uninhabited minor outlying islands with strategic value (Wake, Midway, Johnston, Palmyra).
- **The core problem:** The US federal government that administered these territories has collapsed. There is no successor federal authority to provide governance, defense, constitutional status, or the federal funding that constitutes ~30% of Guam's economy. Their legal status is undefined — they are not independent states, not part of any successor state, and not under international trusteeship. They are constitutional orphans in a geopolitical vacuum.
- **Guam — the critical case:** Guam hosts Andersen Air Force Base and Naval Base Guam — among the most strategically important US military facilities in the Pacific, now orphaned. The bases are contested assets: China wants guarantees of non-use against its interests, the PPR and HFS want to maintain Pacific basing, local Chamorro self-determination movements want the land returned. Guam's economy is ~30% federal spending and ~20% military-related — both disappear with the US collapse. The Chamorro population has a complex relationship with the US (colonized 1898, organic act 1950, but limited self-governance, past US military land seizures). Guamanians are US citizens but geographically isolated from mainland successor states. **Likely trajectory:** Contested transition through the 2030s. The bases become a negotiation point between the PPR, HFS, and China. Guam ultimately affiliates with the PPR or HFS under a compact with significant local autonomy, or pursues full independence (Chamorro sovereignty movement, ~2035-2045).
- **American Samoa:** US nationals (not citizens), unique cultural protections (fa'asamoa system, customary land tenure). No military significance. Smallest and most vulnerable US territory. Likely absorbed by the Hawaiian Free State (cultural connections — both Polynesian) or aligns with independent Samoa.
- **Northern Mariana Islands:** Commonwealth with the US. Tourism-dependent (Japanese and Korean visitors) — collapses with global recession. Geographically close to Asia (north of Guam). Less cultural connection to Polynesia than American Samoa. Most likely to seek alignment with Japan (historical ties, pre-WWII Japanese mandate) or a Pacific regional framework. Chinese economic influence grows as tourism revenue disappears.
- **Minor outlying islands (Wake, Midway, Johnston, Palmyra):** Uninhabited but strategically significant. Wake Island is a Pacific airfield and contested location (claimed by Marshall Islands). Midway is a wildlife refuge with naval history. Johnston was a chemical weapons and nuclear test site. These become administrative responsibilities of the PPR or HFS based on geographic proximity — or contested between them, China, and regional actors.
- **Links to US collapse:** Direct — these territories exist because of the US and collapse with it. Their futures are determined by the power vacuum in the Pacific and the willingness of US successor states to inherit their burdens (federal funding obligations, military infrastructure, self-determination commitments) without the federal revenue that sustained them.

## Revolutionary/Reactionary Dynamics

Oceania's dynamics are shaped by a region where the US collapse removes a security architecture that structured the entire region for 80 years, and where the primary revolutionary mechanism is the "Blue Pacific" regional integration feedback loop.

**The US coercion phase in Oceania (2026-2032):** The dying US did not demand military contributions from Oceania as it did from Asia — the region was too peripheral. But the US did maintain a web of dependencies: ANZUS bound Australia and NZ; AUKUS locked Australia into a US-only technology pipeline; COFA gave the US exclusive military access to Micronesia, Marshall Islands, and Palau; and basing agreements made Australian territory a forward operating base. These were not coercive demands but structural dependencies that limited each state's strategic autonomy.

**The spring effect (2032-2049):** The US collapse removes every one of these dependencies simultaneously. Australia faces the most dramatic reorientation — from US client to independent middle power. The COFA states lose their primary patron and must find new arrangements. NZ barely adjusts. Pacific Island states gain genuine non-alignment for the first time — able to engage China, Australia, Japan, Europe, and US successor states on their own terms.

### Convergent Evolution Trajectories

- **New Zealand stays the course** — the revolutionary small-state model was already correct. NZ remains comfortable, stable, and peripheral, with deeper PPR alignment.
- **Australia pivots revolutionary** — not out of ideological conviction but out of necessity. The US is gone, AUKUS is dead, China is the trading partner, and the only viable path is pragmatic multipolar engagement. The transition is painful — Australian strategic culture has been US-alliance-focused for 80 years — but by the 2040s Australia is an independent middle power in the Asian order, not a US client.
- **"Blue Pacific" regionalism deepens** — the PIF 2050 Strategy becomes the primary framework for Pacific governance. Climate finance, fisheries management, and collective diplomacy give small states influence they could not achieve individually. The Pacific becomes a genuine actor in global affairs, not a passive arena for great-power competition.
- **Atoll states survive legally but not physically** — their maritime zones (200nm EEZs) persist as sovereign territory even as their physical land becomes uninhabitable. A revolutionary legal precedent is set: sovereignty can exist without habitable territory.
- **French Pacific decolonizes** — New Caledonia and French Polynesia gain independence by ~2035-2045. France's Pacific presence ends. The newly independent states join the PIF and align with the "Blue Pacific" framework.
- **PNG follows the reactionary path by default** — governance failure prevents it from capturing the revolutionary potential of its resource wealth and strategic position. It remains a weak, corrupt, aid-dependent state despite its natural endowments.
- **Bougainville achieves independence** if PNG governance continues to fail and if the post-US environment demonstrates that small-state sovereignty is viable. Otherwise it remains an autonomous region.
- **Guam affiliates with the PPR or HFS** after a contested transition period (~2030-2035). The bases become a jointly managed asset — the PPR provides security guarantees, Chamorro self-determination is recognized through a compact with significant local autonomy. Full independence is possible but unlikely — Guam is too small and economically dependent. American Samoa is absorbed into HFS. CNMI aligns with Japan or a Pacific regional framework.
- **Minor outlying islands** are administered by the PPR (Wake, Midway — closest to HFS/PPR territory) or become contested (Palmyra — Johnston — Kingman Reef have less strategic value).

### Cross-Actor Feedback Effects

- **US collapse → all Oceania actors (structural shock):** The removal of the US security architecture (ANZUS, AUKUS, COFA, basing) simultaneously affects every actor. Australia loses its primary strategic framework, the COFA states lose their patron, and Pacific Island states gain genuine non-alignment. The shock is universal but the response differs by prior dependency depth.
- **Australia ↔ China (economic gravity):** Australia's resource exports to China are the region's most important bilateral economic relationship. China's demand for iron ore, LNG, lithium, and rare earths structures Australia's prosperity. Australia cannot afford to confront China, and China needs Australian resources. Their relationship is transactional, not trust-based — but the transaction benefits both sides. Australia's revolutionary pivot is driven by this economic gravity as much as by the US collapse.
- **Pacific regionalism → extra-regional powers (leverage effect):** As the PIF deepens its institutional capacity, extra-regional powers (China, Japan, US successor states, EU) must engage the region on its own terms rather than through bilateral aid dependencies. The 2050 Strategy becomes the framework that external partners must align with, not bypass.
- **Atoll state legal innovation → global implications:** If sovereignty survives territory loss, it sets a precedent that affects maritime boundaries, EEZ claims, and statehood definitions globally. This is a revolutionary legal contribution from the world's smallest states.
- **Australia's choice → Pacific Islands (signaling effect):** If Australia chooses the revolutionary path (BRICS+ engagement, independent defense, genuine Pacific partnership), it validates the Pacific's "Blue Pacific" strategy and deepens Australia-Pacific alignment. If Australia chooses the reactionary path (clinging to US-client model, confronting China), it creates tension with Pacific states that have chosen non-alignment.
- **Guam bases → PPR/HFS/China (contested asset):** Andersen AFB and Naval Base Guam are the most valuable military infrastructure in the Central Pacific. Their ownership and access rights are contested between the PPR (wants Pacific basing), HFS (geographic proximity, cultural connections), and China (wants assurance against use in any future conflict). The resolution of Guam's status determines the balance of military capability in the Western Pacific for decades. If the PPR or HFS secures basing rights, they inherit significant force projection capability. If China gains access guarantees, it neutralizes a key US-era military asset.

## Cross-Regional Dynamics

- **Pacific as strategic theater:** The Pacific Ocean becomes the world's most important strategic theater — connecting China, Japan, Korea, Southeast Asia, Oceania, and the Americas. The US naval dominance that structured Pacific order since 1945 is replaced by a multipolar naval environment. China builds a blue-water navy (already the world's largest by hull count). India expands Indian Ocean presence. Australia, Japan, and regional navies operate independently.
- **Tuna geopolitics:** The Western and Central Pacific Fisheries Commission manages the world's largest tuna fishery (60% of global catch, $5B+/year). Fishing access fees are the most significant source of independent revenue for Pacific Island states. China, Japan, Taiwan, Korea, and US successor states (PPR, Hawaiian Free State) compete for access. The PPR and HFS are natural Pacific fishing powers.
- **Climate migration:** Pacific Island climate migration reshapes the region's demographics. Internal migration (rural to urban), intra-regional (to Fiji, NZ, Australia), and extra-regional (to PPR West Coast). The "climate visa" concept (NZ's Pacific Access Category, Australia's Pacific Engagement Visa) expands but does not meet demand.
- **Hawaiian Free State as Pacific actor:** The Hawaiian Free State becomes an independent Pacific power — a bridge between US successor states and Oceania. Cultural connections to Polynesian states, strategic position in the Central Pacific, infrastructure (Pearl Harbor, Hickam). Joins the PIF as an observer or member.
- **Blue Pacific versus Indo-Pacific:** The "Blue Pacific" framing (Pacific Island-led, regionally owned, sovereign) competes with the "Indo-Pacific" framing (great-power strategic theater). The post-US environment favors Blue Pacific — small states define the region on their own terms rather than being defined by great-power competition.

## Key Uncertainties

- **Australia's strategic choice:** Whether Australia successfully pivots to revolutionary multipolar engagement or remains trapped in reactionary US-client inertia. The AUKUS decision (whether to continue paying for submarines that may never arrive, or cut losses and redirect to independent capability) is the concrete inflection point. **(MEDIUM)**
- **AUKUS collapse fallout:** Whether AUKUS formally collapses, is significantly delayed, or limps along with reduced scope. Each outcome generates different strategic pressure on Australia. The most likely outcome is de facto collapse by ~2030 when US deliveries fail to materialize and Australia quietly pivots. **(MEDIUM)**
- **Pacific Island survival:** Whether atoll states physically survive as sovereign territories with habitable land. Current projections suggest functional uninhabitability by ~2060-2080. The 2026-2049 period is the prelude — legal sovereignty may persist, but physical displacement begins. **(MEDIUM)**
- **COFA transition:** How Micronesia, Palau, and Marshall Islands navigate the post-COFA environment. Whether they find new patrons (China, Australia, regional frameworks) or face a destabilizing gap in aid and security. **(MEDIUM)**
- **Bougainville independence:** Whether Bougainville achieves independence from PNG, and whether it is functional or a resource-curse victim. **(LOW)**
- **New Caledonia outcome:** Whether New Caledonia remains French, becomes independent, or pursues intermediate status. **(LOW)**
- **Papua New Guinea trajectory:** Whether PNG can manage resource wealth and governance to unlock revolutionary potential, or defaults to reactionary resource-curse trajectory. Governance deficit biases toward the latter. **(LOW)**
- **US territories resolution:** Whether Guam's status is resolved through PPR/HFS affiliation, Chamorro independence, or remains contested through the transition. The fate of the military bases (Andersen, Naval Base Guam) is the most consequential strategic question — whoever controls them gains significant Pacific force projection capability. **(MEDIUM)**

## Links to US Successor State System

- The PPR and the Hawaiian Free State are Oceania's primary US successor state partners — both are Pacific-facing, both need Pacific trade routes, both have cultural connections (PPR's Asian-American population, HFS's Polynesian identity)
- The PPR's West Coast ports (LA/Long Beach, Oakland, Seattle/Tacoma) and HFS's Honolulu port are Oceania's primary North American trade gateways
- Australia and NZ have strongest ties to the NEC (historical Commonwealth connections, financial links) but increasingly engage the PPR (technology, climate, Pacific trade)
- US successor states are not Pacific security providers — that role is vacated by the US collapse and filled by China, Australia, and regional actors
- The Hawaiian Free State is uniquely positioned as a Pacific diplomatic actor — small enough to be non-threatening, independent enough to be credible, strategically located enough to be valuable
- Reactionary US successor states (Gulf Compact, Heartland, Atlantic South) have no presence in or relevance to Oceania
- The PPR and HFS are natural participants in the "Blue Pacific" framework — their interests align with Pacific regionalism, not great-power competition
- The US Pacific territories (Guam, American Samoa, CNMI) are the most direct link between the US collapse and Oceania — they are constitutional orphans that must be absorbed or granted self-determination by US successor states, primarily the PPR and HFS
