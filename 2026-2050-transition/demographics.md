# Demographics: Peak, Migration, and Aging

**Domain:** `= this.domain`
**Milestone:** `= this.milestone`
**Status:** `= this.status`

## Key Events

The demographic trajectory from 2026-2049 is defined by three intersecting forces: global population reaching its all-time peak and beginning decline, climate-driven migration becoming a first-order geopolitical variable, and rapid aging in developed and middle-income regions. Within the former US, fragmentation creates new migration regimes between successor states, while climate pressures drive internal and cross-border movement at unprecedented scale.

- **(T-09) Sep 2036 — Climate Migration Exceeds 20 Million** (HIGH): The scale of cross-border climate migration reshapes demographics in both source and destination regions. The 20 million figure represents a tipping point where migration becomes a visible, politically dominant issue globally.
- **(T-13) Nov 2043 — Global Population Peaks** (HIGH): World population peaks at approximately 9.7 billion and begins the long decline of the second half of the century. The peak comes earlier than most 2020s projections as global fertility falls faster than expected.
- **(T-15) Aug 2047 — Amazon Dieback Threshold** (MEDIUM): Agricultural collapse in South America creates a new wave of climate migrants, compounding existing demographic pressures and demonstrating the cascading effects of ecological tipping points on human population distribution.

## Driver Analysis

### Driver 1: Global Fertility Decline and Population Aging

**Confidence:** (HIGH)

**Description:** Total fertility rates fall below replacement level in most world regions. By the 2040s, only Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia maintain above-replacement fertility. The global dependency ratio rises as the working-age population shrinks relative to elders.

**Timeline (2026-2049):**
- 2026-2032: East Asia, Europe, and North America fertility stabilize at sub-replacement levels
- 2033-2039: Latin America and Southeast Asia fall below replacement
- 2040-2043: Global TFR drops below 2.1
- 2044-2049: Population decline begins in most regions

**Linked Events:** T-13

**Cross-Domain Effects:**
- **Economy:** Shrinking workforce, automation pressure
- **Culture:** Aging population values shift toward conservatism and stability

### Driver 2: Climate-Driven Migration Acceleration

**Confidence:** (HIGH)

**Description:** The pace and scale of climate-driven migration grows exponentially through the period. Initial flows (2026-2035) are primarily internal and regional; by 2036-2049 they become cross-border at scale, transforming destination country demographics and politics.

**Timeline (2026-2049):**
- 2026-2032: Climate migration primarily internal (rural to urban)
- 2033-2039: Cross-border flows accelerate past 20M cumulative
- 2040-2045: Multiple climate "hot spots" produce simultaneous migration events
- 2046-2049: Year-round climate migration becomes normal

**Linked Events:** T-09, T-15

**Cross-Domain Effects:**
- **Climate:** Primary migration driver
- **Borders:** Migration pressure at borders of successor states and between nations
- **Culture:** Nativist vs. cosmopolitan ideological conflict

### Driver 3: US Successor State Demographic Divergence

**Confidence:** (MEDIUM)

**Description:** The former United States' regions have different fertility rates, age structures, and migration patterns. Successor states inherit these demographic profiles and develop divergent population policies, creating demographic winners and losers.

**Timeline (2026-2049):**
- 2026-2032: Regional demographic divergence already evident (aging Northeast vs. younger South/West)
- 2033-2040: Successor states implement different migration and fertility policies
- 2041-2049: Demographic trajectories diverge sharply — Pacific Republic attracts young educated migrants, Heartland ages fastest

**Linked Events:** T-08, T-10, T-11, T-13

**Cross-Domain Effects:**
- **Borders:** Demographic pressures influence border policy
- **Economy:** Labor shortages in some successor states drive automation

### Driver 4: Urbanization and Coastal Abandonment

**Confidence:** (MEDIUM)

**Description:** The long-term trend of urbanization continues but shifts direction — coastal cities face managed retreat due to sea level rise, while interior cities absorb displaced populations. Megacities in the global South grow explosively despite climate vulnerability.

**Timeline (2026-2049):**
- 2026-2032: Coastal real estate markets begin pricing in climate risk
- 2033-2039: Major coastal cities begin systematic retreat programs (Miami, NYC, Shanghai, Mumbai, Lagos)
- 2040-2049: Interior "climate haven" cities experience rapid growth and infrastructure strain

**Linked Events:** T-04, T-09

**Cross-Domain Effects:**
- **Economy:** Trillions in coastal asset destruction
- **Climate:** The retreat itself is a response to climate drivers

## Cross-Domain Effects

- **To Economy:** Labor force declines in aged regions drive automation adoption and change consumption patterns.
- **To Borders:** Climate migration creates border enforcement challenges; successor states use demographic policy to define membership.
- **To Culture:** Demographic change drives cultural change; migration creates contact between different value systems; population decline alters national psychology.

## Known Uncertainties

- **Fertility cliff:** Global fertility could fall faster (approaching 1.5 global TFR by 2050) if urbanization and education trends accelerate in Sub-Saharan Africa. **(LOW)**
- **Climate migration scale:** The World Bank Groundswell estimates 216M internal migrants by 2050 under pessimistic scenario; cross-border fraction is highly uncertain. **(MEDIUM)**
- **US successor state population policy:** Could range from open immigration (Pacific Republic) to restrictive/nationalist (Gulf Compact), with large demographic consequences. **(MEDIUM)**
- **Longevity breakthroughs:** Significant lifespan extension via biotech would upend all demographic projections — a 2040s breakthrough changes the entire picture. **(LOW)**
