## Key Changes From Previous Milestone

- US federal dissolution and emergence of ~26 successor-state-level entities across former US territory, organized along a revolutionary/reactionary axis with a cross-cutting indigenous sovereignty dimension
- Indigenous sovereignty achieved by at least 5 nations within former US borders — Navajo, Dakota/Lakota, Sequoyan/Cherokee, Tlingit Aaní, and Haudenosaunee Confederacy — each with varying degrees of de facto independence
- BRICS+ alternative financial system fully operational, multicurrency reserve standard replacing dollar dominance, and the post-American international order consolidated around multipolar institutional frameworks
  - At least 4 US successor states and/or indigenous sovereign nations recognized by a majority of UN member states, with revolutionary states (Pacifica, NEC, GLPR, Aztlán) forming the stable core of the post-American system
- West Asia reconfigured around the Iran-Turkey-Saudi Arabia-Pakistan Quartet security framework, with Israel territorially contracted to a nuclear-armed coastal rump state and Palestine established as a contiguous sovereign entity
- Every British, Dutch, and French overseas territory in the Caribbean achieved full sovereignty and CARICOM membership — the European colonial presence in the Caribbean ended as the US collapse removed the Monroe Doctrine and dollar-denominated financial infrastructure that sustained the offshore territory model. CARICOM deepened from a trade bloc into a genuine regional governance framework with free movement, a supranational court, and collective security coordination. Full political unification assessed as a plausible trajectory by ~2075.

## Analysis

### Former United States Territory

The territory of the former United States is occupied by ~26 successor-state-level entities organized along a revolutionary/reactionary axis with a cross-cutting indigenous sovereignty dimension. Revolutionary states — those that deprivatized major industries, adopted state-directed economic planning, and incorporated Landback into founding ideology — collectively control ~58% of the former US population and ~65% of its GDP. Reactionary states — those that clung to nationalist-capitalist/extractive models — are in various stages of collapse or revolutionary transition.

#### Revolutionary States

**Pacifica:** Governs ~55M citizens across the former West Coast states of California, Oregon, Washington (west of the Cascades), and western Nevada. Pacifica is the most stable and economically viable successor state, with a democratic-socialist economy generating ~$2.5T in GDP. Strong technology, green energy, and entertainment sectors. Autonomous indigenous zones established for Coast Salish, Puyallup, and other nations within its territory. Pacifica maintains friendly relations with the Hawaiian Free State, Tlingit Aaní, and the Front Range Socialist Republic. **→ See KML: Pacifica** **→ See transition doc: [successor-states.md](../../2026-2050-transition/successor-states.md#1-pacific-peoples-republic-ppr)**

**Hawaiian Free State (HFS):** Independent sovereign nation with ~1.4M citizens across the full Hawaiian Islands. Democratic-socialist economy built on converted military infrastructure, renewable energy (geothermal, solar, wind), and Pacific trade hub functions. Native Hawaiian sovereignty forms the constitutional foundation, with Aloha ʻāina as the governing principle. Major Pacific diplomatic actor — maintains close alliance with the Pacifica but did not merge. **→ See KML: Hawaiian Free State**

**Northeast Corridor (NEC):** Governs ~55M citizens across the Boston-to-Washington megalopolis. Social-democratic mixed economy. Finance sector retained but transformed post-dollar-collapse, education (Ivy League), healthcare, and professional services drive the economy. Strong ties to the European Union and the United Kingdom. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy operates as an autonomous indigenous zone within NEC territory. **→ See KML: Northeast Corridor**

**Great Lakes People's Republic (GLPR):** Governs ~50M citizens across Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Iowa, Missouri, and western Pennsylvania. The manufacturing-driven economy (~$440B+ combined industrial base) is the anchor of the revolutionary bloc in the interior. Reindustrialization through public ownership of strategic industries, with worker cooperatives in smaller enterprises. The GLPR controls Great Lakes shipping, the St. Lawrence Seaway, and the Mississippi River corridor. **→ See KML: Great Lakes People's Republic**

**Front Range Socialist Republic (FRSR):** Governs ~5M citizens along the Colorado Front Range corridor (Denver/Boulder/Fort Collins/Colorado Springs). Viable but strained — high defense spending as a revolutionary island surrounded by reactionary territory, water constraints from the Colorado River system, and landlocked logistics. Strong technology, aerospace (Space Command/NORAD legacy), and renewable energy sectors. Allied with the Pacifica and GLPR via air and trade corridors. **→ See KML: Front Range Socialist Republic**

**Aztlán:** The northern revolutionary autonomous region of the unified Mexican republic, governing ~15M citizens across Arizona (minus Navajo Nation), New Mexico (minus Navajo Nation), and the Hispanic-majority counties of Texas — a continuous territorial corridor from the Permian Basin through the Rio Grande Valley, sweeping north to encompass San Antonio (Bexar County) and the majority-Hispanic counties of Medina, Uvalde, Caldwell, and Atascosa, then following the Nueces River to Corpus Christi. The border cuts through central Texas along demographic lines — Hispanic-majority south, Anglo-majority north — with San Antonio as the region's urban anchor. A renewable energy superpower — the best solar resource in the former US — and anchor of semiconductor manufacturing (Phoenix TSMC/Intel legacy). The Permian Basin's hydrocarbon infrastructure has been repurposed for geothermal and grid-scale storage. Water-constrained but managed via desalination partnerships with Mexico and aggressive conservation. Socialist economic model with indigenous autonomous zones for Navajo and other nations. Founds itself on Aztlán nationalism fused with socialist economics — the original Chicano Socialist Republic (CSR) project evolved from separatist to integrative, culminating in formal unification with Mexico by the late 2040s. **→ See KML: Aztlán**

**New Afrika:** A Black-led revolutionary socialist republic governing ~23M across Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia, South Carolina, and the Florida Panhandle. The Republic of New Afrika emerged from the internal revolution (~2044-2046) that overthrew the revived CSA. Jackson, Mississippi serves as the capital. The economy is rebuilding from the brutal CSA period — Gulf shipping through New Orleans, Mobile, Savannah, and Charleston; agriculture (cotton, rice, soybeans); and manufacturing are being reorganized under public ownership. Trade unions, illegal under the CSA, have organized explosively since the revolution. New Afrika respects indigenous sovereignty with nation-to-nation relationships with Choctaw, Coushatta, Poarch Creek, Catawba, and other nations within its territory. Strong ties to the GLPR and NEC. Emerging as the revolutionary anchor of the Southeast. **→ See KML: New Afrika**

**Alleghenia:** A revolutionary republic governing ~5M across the Greater Pittsburgh region — 37 counties in western Pennsylvania plus the WV northern panhandle (Hancock, Brooke, Ohio, Marshall). Alleghenia broke away from the US remnant government ~2038-2040, after NEC and GLPR had already declared independence. Pittsburgh's strong labor history and post-industrial innovation economy (robotics, AI, healthcare) create a distinct revolutionary identity — neither NEC nor GLPR, but allied with both. Controls critical infrastructure: Marcellus/Utica shale gas, Pittsburgh manufacturing, and the Ohio River corridor. Alleghenia bridges the GLPR and NEC geographically and economically. **→ See KML: Alleghenia**

**Appalachia:** A revolutionary corridor governing ~2M across all of West Virginia (minus eastern panhandle to Virginia; northern panhandle to Alleghenia), ~15 western Virginia counties, and 24 eastern Kentucky coal counties. The first area in the South to revolt against CSA rule, using mountainous terrain and the dormant United Mine Workers organizing tradition to expel CSA forces by ~2044. West Virginia state identity was subsumed during this process — the state government dissolved by ~2041, and WV no longer exists as a political unit. Appalachia is explicitly anti-CSA — West Virginia was never Confederate, born from the anti-secessionist Restored Government of Virginia. Functions as a revolutionary corridor linking the GLPR, Alleghenia, and New Afrika. Mountainous terrain makes it highly defensible. Not self-sufficient in food or energy but strategically positioned astride the Appalachian spine. **→ See KML: Appalachia**

#### Indigenous Sovereign Nations

**Navajo Nation (Diné Bikéyah):** Sovereign indigenous socialist republic allied to Aztlán and Pacifica, population ~500K. Governs 27,425 sq miles spanning Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah. Strong institutional governance — tripartite government, 110 chapters, sovereign tax authority. Economy transitioning from fossil fuels to solar energy. Strong cultural/national identity. **→ See KML: Navajo Nation**

**Dakota/Lakota Nation (Oceti Sakowin):** Independent indigenous revolutionary confederation governing all of North Dakota and South Dakota, with territory extending into northeastern Nebraska. Population ~250K (growing — diaspora repatriation driven by Landback and cultural revitalization). The Oceti Sakowin declared full sovereignty over the Fort Laramie treaty territories at T-18 (~2036-2037), carving the heart out of the Heartland reactionary territory. By 2050, the indigenous model has consolidated across both Dakotas with a cooperative economy based on wind energy, bison restoration, and renewable energy cooperatives. **→ See KML: Dakota/Lakota Nation**

**Sequoyan Nation (Cherokee):** Sovereign indigenous revolutionary confederation of the Five Tribes — Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek (Muscogee), and Seminole — governing eastern and south-central Oklahoma. Population ~1M citizens across 40 counties. The Sequoyah confederation emerged from the dissolution of Oklahoma's state institutions (~2038-2040) as each of the Five Tribes asserted sovereignty, then federated into a single indigenous polity. Strong diversified economy — healthcare, education, energy, manufacturing, gaming. Professional confederal governance with a council of the Five Tribes. Treaty relations established with Aztlán and economic ties to the GLPR and NEC. **→ See KML: Sequoyan Nation**

**Tlingit Aaní:** Independent indigenous confederation governing southeast Alaska (panhandle — Juneau, Sitka, Ketchikan, Inside Passage). Population ~75K. Strong fishing, hydroelectricity, and timber economy. Socialist governance model — decentralized, indigenous-led. Pacifica-friendly but independent. No contiguous land connection to the Lower 48. **→ See KML: Tlingit Aaní**

**Haudenosaunee Confederacy:** Autonomous indigenous zone within the Northeast Corridor. The oldest living democracy in North America — founded ~1142 CE. Operates its own passports, courts, and diplomatic relationships. NEC's revolutionary ideology accommodates Haudenosaunee sovereignty within its territory.

#### Reactionary States

**Texas:** A reactionary rump governing ~30M across the remainder of Texas — the territory east and north of the Aztlán corridor. Anchored by the Dallas/Fort Worth, Houston, and San Antonio metro regions. The energy industry's collapse and the loss of the Permian Basin and coastal refinery strip to Aztlán have hollowed the state's traditional economic base. Houston's petrochemical corridor is a contested zone — organized workers push revolutionary while the Dallas and San Antonio political establishments hold reactionary. Texas is structurally landlocked between Aztlán to the south/west, New Afrika to the east, and the Heartland/Oceti Sakowin to the north. Borders New Afrika along the Sabine River. **→ See KML: Texas**

**Florida:** A degraded reactionary rump governing ~12M across the Florida mainland — minus the Panhandle, which joined New Afrika. Tourism is severely diminished by hurricane risk and extreme heat; citrus agriculture is declining from saltwater intrusion. Miami increasingly operates as a semi-independent city-state oriented toward CARICOM trade. The Panhandle's loss and the CSA's collapse shattered Florida's identity as a coherent state. Interior Florida is hollowing. **→ See KML: Florida**

**North Carolina:** A hollowed reactionary rump governing ~8M across North Carolina — minus the Research Triangle Area (Raleigh-Durham-Chapel Hill), which exists as a besieged revolutionary city-state tenuously linked to New Afrika and the NEC. Agriculture (tobacco, soybeans, hogs), furniture, and textiles in decline. Charlotte's finance sector collapsed with the dollar. The state's identity is defined by what it lost — RTP, economic relevance, and any coherent purpose. **→ See KML: North Carolina**

**Virginia (Southern Rump):** A tiny reactionary rump governing ~4M across southern Virginia — everything south of the NEC border (roughly the Fredericksburg line). Northern Virginia / NOVA is NEC territory. Agriculture and residual military infrastructure (Hampton Roads) in decline without federal funding. The rump is defined by what it lost — NOVA, the federal presence, and any institutional capacity. **→ See KML: Virginia**

**Tennessee:** A reactionary rump governing ~5M across the state of Tennessee. Agriculture, healthcare (Nashville legacy), and logistics in decline without national economic integration. Nashville's music/entertainment industry collapsed with the national market. Its historic position as a crossroads state became irrelevant when the roads led through hostile territory — New Afrika to the south, the Appalachia to the east. **→ See KML: Tennessee**

**Kentucky:** A small reactionary rump governing ~3M across Kentucky — minus the eastern coal counties that broke away to form part of the Appalachia. Bourbon, horses, tobacco, and agriculture in decline without access to national distribution. No significant urban centers, no ports, no strategic resources. One of the weakest reactionary entities in the former US. **→ See KML: Kentucky**

**Heartland:** A shrinking reactionary rump governing Nebraska, Kansas, and the eastern Colorado plains, surrounded by revolutionary states — the GLPR to the east, Oceti Sakowin to the north. The Dakotas, Iowa, and Missouri were lost to indigenous sovereignty and GLPR absorption during the 2036-2040 fragmentation. Economically dependent and politically unstable. Export agriculture base was strangled by landlocked logistics controlled by revolutionary neighbors. **→ See KML: Heartland**

**Arkansas:** A weak reactionary rump governing the territory of the former state of Arkansas. Population ~3M. Agricultural economy (rice, soybeans, poultry) and logistics (Mississippi River corridor) in decline without federal subsidies. Centered on Little Rock. The collapse of federal governance left Arkansas's institutions intact but hollowed — the state functions as a reactionary remnant with minimal capacity for collective action. Contains the Walmart-Bentonville corridor which operates as a semi-autonomous corporate governance zone. Status provisional — the broader reconfiguration of the US South may reassign Arkansas's territory. **→ See KML: Arkansas**

**Oklahoma:** A reactionary rump governing the western half of the former state — the Oklahoma City metro area, the Panhandle, and the western agricultural plains. Population ~2M. Oil and gas economy in terminal decline; agricultural base (wheat, cattle) automated and export-dependent. Centered on Oklahoma City. The state was severed from its eastern half by the Sequoyah indigenous confederation during the ~2038-2040 fragmentation and is not viable as an independent entity — its survival depends on trade access through GLPR- and Sequoyah-controlled corridors. Contains autonomous zones for non-Five-Tribes nations (Osage, Cheyenne-Arapaho, Kiowa-Comanche-Apache). **→ See KML: Oklahoma**

**Deseret:** The reactionary Mormon state — culturally intact but economically hollow by 2050. Population ~6M across Utah (minus Navajo-overlap counties), southern Idaho, and eastern Nevada. The LDS Church provides institutional backbone and social safety net, but the tech sector withered without global integration and mining/extraction declined with the energy transition. Young people continue to leave for revolutionary states. Indigenous sovereignty in southern Utah (Navajo lands) is recognized. **→ See KML: Deseret**

**Columbia:** An Inland Northwest republic governing ~2.8M across eastern Washington, eastern Oregon, and the northern Idaho panhandle. Anchored by the Columbia and Snake River watersheds with a hydroelectric and agricultural economic base. The Spokane metro area (~600K) serves as the capital. Initially a reactionary agricultural breakaway, the Columbia Republic has been flipping revolutionary through organized labor in the hydroelectric and logistics sectors, driven by Cascadian influence and internal organizing. **→ See KML: Columbia**

**Absaroka:** A reactionary mountain state in the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem governing ~700K across western Montana, northwest Wyoming, and the eastern Idaho highlands. Anchored by Bozeman as capital. The economy depends on tourism, wildlife conservation, and resource extraction in decline. Absaroka is defined by its ecological identity — one of the last intact temperate-zone ecosystems on Earth, with the continental triple divide within its territory. Sparsely populated, heavily armed, and resistant to outside authority. **→ See KML: Absaroka**

**Montana-Wyoming:** A reactionary plains rump governing ~1.5M across eastern Montana and central-eastern Wyoming. Ranching and declining fossil energy (coal, oil, gas) form a shrinking economic base. Anchored by Billings as capital. Functionally a buffer state between the revolutionary interior (GLPR, Oceti Sakowin) and the mountain states. Increasingly dependent on trade with stronger neighbors. **→ See KML: Montana-Wyoming**

**Colorado:** A fragmented headwaters state governing ~800K across the Western Slope of Colorado — the rural territory west of the Continental Divide. Anchored by Grand Junction as capital. The defining strategic asset is control of the Colorado River's headwaters in a water-scarce future. Ski country, energy transition, and water politics dominate. Functions as a tense buffer between the revolutionary Front Range Socialist Republic and the reactionary interior. **→ See KML: Colorado**

#### Degrading Rumps

**Alaska:** A shrunken, impoverished rump around Anchorage with ~450K population. Oil revenue collapsed, the Permanent Fund depleted, and supply lines from the mainland severed after the federal rump collapse. Indigenous territories (North Slope, Western Alaska, Interior) effectively separated. Some residual military infrastructure but no capacity to use it. **→ See KML: Alaska**

**Alaska Indigenous Mosaic:** A mosaic of indigenous-led territories (North Slope Iñupiat, Western Yupik, Interior Athabascan, Aleut/Unangax̂, Alutiiq/Sugpiaq) outside Anchorage control. Varying sizes and capacities — some highly functional (North Slope with oil wealth), some struggling. Governed through ANCSA corporation structures and tribal governments. **→ See KML: Tlingit Aaní**

### North America (Beyond Former US)

**Canada:** Survives as a federation through deep regional reconfiguration — battered, poorer, but intact. A decentralized federation: Quebec holds enhanced autonomy (third referendum resulted in negotiated asymmetric federalism rather than exit), Alberta wields outsized influence through resource leverage, British Columbia functions as Canada's Pacific bridge with deep Cascadian economic integration along the Vancouver-Seattle-Portland corridor, and Atlantic Canada orients eastward toward the NEC and Europe. The 5,500-mile US-Canada border is now a complex multi-jurisdictional frontier. **→ See KML: Canada** **→ See transition doc: [north-america.md](../../2026-2050-transition/regions/north-america.md#1-canada)**

**Mexico:** A unified revolutionary republic incorporating Aztlán as its northern autonomous socialist region. The Chicano Socialist Republic's original separatist project evolved into formal unification by the late 2040s — Aztlán's revolutionary energy and renewable energy infrastructure (semiconductor manufacturing, desert solar farms) integrated into Mexico's broader left-governance framework. The northern border was reconfigured from a single US-Mexico boundary into a complex multi-jurisdictional frontier between Mexico-Aztlán and the fragmented US successor states (New Afrika, Texas, Pacifica, Front Range). Deepened ties with Brazil and BRICS+. A middle-income transitional economy trending toward developmental socialism. **→ See KML: Mexico**

### Caribbean

The Caribbean in 2050 has undergone a fundamental transformation: every overseas territory of European powers — British, Dutch, and French — is now a fully sovereign independent state and CARICOM member. This is not gradual decolonization but a revolutionary decolonization-through-regional-integration, accelerated by the US collapse (~2030s) which removed the Monroe Doctrine and the dollar-denominated financial system that kept offshore territories as passive appendages of European metropoles. CARICOM itself has deepened from a trading bloc into a genuine regional governance framework — free movement of people, capital, and labor is operational (pioneered by Barbados, Belize, Dominica, and St. Vincent in 2025); the Caribbean Court of Justice (CCJ) is the final appellate authority for most members; and collective foreign and security policy coordination has advanced. Full political unification remains beyond the 2050 horizon but is assessed as a plausible trajectory by ~2075 — driven by climate existential threat (sea-level rise, hurricane intensification), economic necessity (no single island can compete in a BRICS+ world), and the accumulated institutional trust built through CARICOM's deepening. The spectrum of revolutionary commitment is wide — from Cuba's veteran socialist project to the former offshore financial centers adapting to a post-dollar world — but all 28 entities are on the CARICOM track, and none remain as European colonial remnants.

**Cuba:** The anchor of Caribbean revolutionary socialism. Post-US-collapse normalization with Aztlán and NEC transformed Cuba from a besieged outlier into the region's diplomatic hub — hosting BRICS+ engagement, medical diplomacy, and technology transfer. The US embargo collapsed with the federal government; Cuba's pharmaceutical and biotech sectors, long developed in isolation, became valuable export industries. Tourism reoriented toward BRICS+ markets (China, Russia, Brazil). Deepened defense cooperation with Venezuela and the EAF. **→ See KML: Cuba**

**Haiti:** The region's deepest crisis. Catastrophic humanitarian conditions — failed state, gang control of Port-au-Prince, cholera resurgence, deforestation-driven landslides. CARICOM's collective security framework has been forced to engage Haiti as a regional stability problem, with a CARICOM stabilization mission operating since the late 2030s. No single island can solve Haiti; it is the primary test of whether CARICOM can function as a genuine security community. **→ See KML: Haiti**

**Dominican Republic:** The Hispaniola dual-state dynamic inverted — the DR, historically the more stable half, absorbed massive Haitian refugee flows and experienced its own destabilization. The border hardened after the ~2035 CARICOM stabilization mission, with the DR maintaining autonomy from deeper CARICOM security integration while participating in the economic framework. Tends conservative and defensive — the region's most reactionary pole after the offshore centers. **→ See KML: Dominican Rep.**

**Jamaica:** The historical heavyweight — independence leader, WIF skeptic (its 1961 withdrawal killed the first unification attempt), regional cultural superpower (reggae, dancehall, global diaspora). In 2050, Jamaica has evolved from WIF-trauma isolationism into a cautious CARICOM integrator — still wary of subsidizing smaller islands (the "big island problem" that killed the 1962 Federation), but without a US patron to hedge against, CARICOM is the only game in town. Leads on CARICOM financial services reform and diaspora engagement. Lean-revolutionary; cultural influence outweighs economic weight. **→ See KML: Jamaica**

**Trinidad and Tobago:** The region's petrostate. Hydrocarbon wealth (natural gas, petrochemicals, asphalt) gave Trinidad outsized economic weight but also created a structural tension — Trinidad broke ranks with CARICOM consensus on "Zone of Peace" issues in prior decades (welcoming US military operations while other members opposed). Post-collapse, Trinidad is a swing state: its energy exports are critical to regional energy security, but its elite remains the most resistant to CARICOM's revolutionary trajectory. The "one from ten leaves nought" fear persists — Trinidad and Jamaica together could block unification indefinitely. **→ See KML: Trinidad and Tobago**

**Puerto Rico:** De facto independence achieved by default (~2035-2040) after the US federal collapse — no active secession struggle, simply the metropolitan patron disintegrated. Puerto Rico absorbed the US Virgin Islands in the chaos, forming a merged PRI+VIR entity (~3.5M population). The new state faces an existential economic transition: severed from US federal transfers (Medicare, Medicaid, food stamps, infrastructure subsidies — previously 30%+ of GDP) and the bankruptcy-restructured debt overhang from the pre-collapse era. Oriented toward CARICOM associate membership and BRICS+ alignment, but the transition from US colony to independent CARICOM state is a generation-long project. Puerto Rico is the living laboratory of whether US-collapse-born states can successfully integrate into the post-American world. **→ See KML: Puerto Rico**

**Bahamas:** The premier offshore financial center pre-collapse. The dollar collapse (~2030s) destroyed the offshore financial model — Bahamian banks and trust structures denominated in US dollars became liabilities overnight. Rapid reorientation toward euro, yuan, and cryptocurrency-based finance. Population of ~400K spread across 700 islands made economic diversification difficult. Tourism reoriented toward European and BRICS+ markets. Deeply CARICOM-committed — no alternative patron exists. The Bahamas' sovereignty is defensive; its revolutionary character is economic necessity rather than ideological commitment. **→ See KML: Bahamas**

**Barbados:** The political and institutional leader of the Caribbean revolutionary integration project. Barbados led the 2025 free-movement pioneer group, hosts the CCJ, and functions as CARICOM's administrative center. Post-collapse, Barbadian diplomacy oriented the entire region toward BRICS+ engagement. A functional social democracy with high human development, strong institutions, and a pragmatic revolutionary character. The republic transition (removing the British monarch as head of state, 2021) foreshadowed the broader Caribbean break from European structures. **→ See KML: Barbados**

**Offshore Territories Flipped (the UK/Dutch/French overseas territories, now sovereign CARICOM members):**

**Cayman Islands:** The largest remaining offshore financial center — ~$1.5T in banking liabilities pre-collapse. The dollar collapse was an existential shock; Cayman reoriented toward multi-currency financial services (euro, yuan, crypto). A tax-neutral jurisdiction by legacy, now facing pressure to contribute to CARICOM collective finance. Population ~80K. **→ See KML: Cayman Islands**

**British Virgin Islands:** Second-tier offshore center (after Cayman), specializing in corporate registrations rather than banking. The dollar collapse had similar devastating effects. BVI reoriented toward Asian financial flows. The UK's post-collapse inability to maintain territorial governance (withdrew to European defense perimeter by ~2035) forced BVI to fend for itself — CARICOM membership was the natural successor. **→ See KML: British Virgin Is.**

**Turks and Caicos Islands:** Tourism-dependent archipelago of 40 islands (~50K population). The "Turkish" identity (named after Turks Cay, not Turkey) is a quirk of colonial cartography. Post-collapse, TCI leveraged its geography — Atlantic approaches, proximity to the Bahamas and Dominican Republic — as a transshipment and logistics node. Moderate revolutionary trajectory, less ideologically committed than the eastern Caribbean states. **→ See KML: Turks and Caicos Is.**

**Anguilla:** The smallest CARICOM member (~15K population). Independence from the UK was functional default — the British withdrew, and Anguilla simply continued existing as a sovereign entity. Fishery economics, remittance-dependent. Deeply integrated into the eastern Caribbean sub-regional structures (OECS). **→ See KML: Anguilla**

**Montserrat:** A scar of colonial environmental violence. The Soufrière Hills eruption (1995-1997, ongoing low-level activity) destroyed Plymouth, the capital, and rendered the southern half of the island an exclusion zone. Population ~5K (down from 13K pre-eruption). The British withdrawal left Montserrat as an independent state managing a long-term volcanic crisis with limited resources. CARICOM collective disaster response is existential for Montserrat's survival. **→ See KML: Montserrat**

**Curaçao (ABW+CUW merged):** The merged entity of Aruba and Curaçao within the former Dutch Caribbean — a single state of ~280K. Willemstad (Curaçao) is the political and economic center; Aruba's tourism sector provided complementary revenue. The Kingdom of the Netherlands dissolved its Caribbean constituent-country arrangements (~2030s) as the Netherlands oriented inward to European defense. Curaçao's oil refining and transshipment infrastructure (Venezuelan crude processing) positioned it as an energy logistics hub for the region. Tends pragmatic, pro-CARICOM integration, leveraging its Dutch institutional legacy (civil law, bilingual Papiamentu/English/Dutch population). **→ See KML: Curaçao**

**Saint Martin (SXM+MAF merged):** The merged entity of Sint Maarten (Dutch) and Saint-Martin (French) — the only land border in the Caribbean between two European powers, now erased. A single state of ~75K on a 37-square-mile island. The hurricane destruction of Irma (2017) and the subsequent slow reconstruction exposed the incapacity of both European metropoles to manage Caribbean territory — a key driver of the flip to sovereignty. Tourism economy, duty-free retail, cultural fusion. The merger was organic — the border was always invisible in daily life. **→ See KML: Saint Martin**

**French Overseas Territories Flipped (French overseas departments and collectivities, now sovereign CARICOM members):**

**Martinique:** The largest of the flipped French territories (~360K population). A former overseas department of France (since 1946) and integral part of the French Republic — its separation required a constitutional rupture after the US collapse removed France's capacity to maintain Caribbean governance. Martinique's economy was heavily dependent on French transfers (~€2B/yr in EU/French structural funds), which ended with France's inward turn. The transition to independent CARICOM statehood was traumatic but generated a new economic model — Martinique became the eastern Caribbean's education and health services hub, leveraging its university (UA) and medical infrastructure. Fort-de-France functions as a regional logistics node. Deep integration with Dominica and Saint Lucia forms a Windward Islands sub-regional bloc. **→ See KML: Martinique**

**Guadeloupe:** An archipelago of six inhabited islands (Basse-Terre, Grande-Terre, Marie-Galante, Les Saintes, La Désirade, Saint-Barthélemy until 2007 separation) with ~380K population. Like Martinique, Guadeloupe was an integral overseas department of France, and its separation followed the same dynamic of French withdrawal. Guadeloupe's economic transition was harder than Martinique's — the banana and sugar industries, premised on EU-subsidized access, collapsed without French/EU support. Tourism and renewable energy (geothermal, solar) became the new economic base. Pointe-à-Pitre functions as a commercial hub for the Leeward Islands. Guadeloupe maintains a distinct Creole cultural identity and a history of labor and anti-colonial activism (the 1802 slave revolt, the 1967 riots, the 2009 general strike). **→ See KML: Guadeloupe**

**St. Barthélemy:** The smallest and most elite of the flipped French territories (~10K population). Known as "St. Barth" or "St. Barts," the island was a Swedish colony (1784-1878) returned to France for a debt payment — a colonial trivia that foreshadowed its peculiar trajectory. In the French era, St. Barths was an exclusive luxury tourism destination (yachting, celebrity vacations, high-end retail). Its sovereignty after French withdrawal was nearly seamless — the island's small population, its functioning municipal government, and its wealth insulated it from the transition shock suffered by Martinique and Guadeloupe. St. Barthélemy is the Caribbean's Monaco — a microstate luxury enclave, now aligned with CARICOM but functionally autonomous and tax-competitive. **→ See KML: St. Barthélemy**

**Dutch Caribbean Special Municipalities Flipped (Bonaire, Saba, St. Eustatius — the "BES Islands," now sovereign CARICOM members):**

Bonaire, Saba, and Sint Eustatius were special municipalities of the Netherlands — integrated parts of the European country of the Netherlands (unlike Aruba, Curaçao, and Sint Maarten which were constituent countries of the Kingdom). Their status as municipalities of a European state made the transition to independence legally complex — they were not overseas territories but legal wards of the Dutch state. The Netherlands' post-collapse inward defense orientation (the EU Core Federation absorbed Dutch national governance) created a sovereignty vacuum that the BES Islands filled through CARICOM membership. The BES Islands declared independence jointly as the "Caribbean Netherlands Confederation" — a micro-federation of three islands with a combined population of ~30K — to negotiate collectively with CARICOM and the BRICS+ world.

**Bonaire:** The largest of the BES Islands (~20K population). A diving tourism destination (world-class coral reefs, Bonaire National Marine Park) and a salt production center. The island's economy reoriented from Dutch-subsidized public services to eco-tourism, fisheries, and a small offshore finance sector. Kralendijk is the confederal administrative center. **→ See KML: Bonaire**

**Saba:** The smallest (~2K population) and most striking — a dormant volcanic peak rising directly from the Caribbean Sea, with the region's only airstrip that is among the shortest in the world. Saba's economy is micro-tourism, medical school (Saba University School of Medicine — a significant hard-currency earner), and fishing. The island was a Dutch municipality, but its small scale and self-sufficient tradition made the sovereignty transition manageable. **→ See KML: Saba**

**St. Eustatius:** The "Statia" or "Golden Rock" — an island of strategic history (the first foreign entity to recognize the United States, 1776) and current significance (~3K population). A deepwater port, oil terminal (transshipping crude from Venezuela and elsewhere), and logistics hub. St. Eustatius's economic importance to the BES Confederation exceeds its population size — the terminal generates the bulk of the confederation's revenue. The island's history as a free port in the colonial era prefigured its re-emergence as a CARICOM micro-state trade node. **→ See KML: St. Eustatius**

**Eastern Caribbean OECS Core (the islands that pushed deepest into integration):**

**Antigua and Barbuda:** A middle-weight eastern Caribbean state (~90K). Tourism-dependent but with strategic ambitions — hosts the American University of Antigua (medical school), and pursued financial services sector development. Active CARICOM diplomat, hosting secretariat functions. Barbuda was evacuated post-Irma and remains partially depopulated — climate relocation within a sovereign state, a prefiguration of regional climate migration patterns. **→ See KML: Antigua and Barb.**

**Dominica:** The most radical of the eastern Caribbean revolutionary states. "Nature Island" branding masks a deep-seated rejection of colonial extraction models. Dominica pursued citizenship-by-investment, geothermal energy development (exporting to Martinique and Guadeloupe via undersea cable), and was the earliest and most vocal advocate for deeper CARICOM integration — free-movement pioneer in 2025. Climate-vulnerable (Hurricane Maria 2017 destroyed 226% of GDP in a few hours) but institutionally resilient. **→ See KML: Dominica**

**Grenada:** The "Spice Isle" — nutmeg, mace, cocoa, and a revolutionary history (the 1979-1983 People's Revolutionary Government under Maurice Bishop, crushed by the US invasion). Bishop's legacy is reclaimed in 2050 as the prototype of Caribbean revolutionary socialism — the first state to attempt a Leninist-inspired Caribbean transformation, destroyed by US intervention. In the post-American world, Grenada is completing the revolution that the US invasion interrupted. Leading on CARICOM agricultural policy and food sovereignty. **→ See KML: Grenada**

**Saint Lucia:** A stable parliamentary democracy (~180K). Tourism and banana exports, transitioning toward services and financial services. Moderate pro-CARICOM trajectory — not revolutionary but solidly committed to the integration project. The most French-influenced of the English-speaking islands (Patwa/French Creole majority). **→ See KML: Saint Lucia**

**St. Kitts and Nevis:** The two-island federation (~55K). Nevis retains autonomy and periodic secession rumblings — the only Caribbean state where internal fragmentation is a live issue. Citizenship-by-investment program (sugar industry died in the 2000s) was a major revenue source before the dollar collapse; now oriented toward BRICS+ investment citizenship. **→ See KML: St. Kitts and Nevis**

**St. Vincent and the Grenadines:** The "Little Archipelago" — 32 islands and cays (~110K). The most outspoken voice for Caribbean revolutionary sovereignty. Prime Minister Ralph Gonsalves (in power from 2001 deep into the 2030s) was the region's most articulate left-nationalist intellectual — his critiques of CSME inequities and colonial holdovers shaped the post-collapse CARICOM agenda. The Grenadines chain is a yachting tourism hub; mainland St. Vincent is agricultural (arrowroot, bananas). Hosts a CARICOM defense coordination center. **→ See KML: St. Vin. and Gren.**

Brazil is socialist-leaning social-democratic, aligned with the multipolar bloc. The military, internally divided and without US backing, is no longer a political actor. **→ See KML: Brazil**

Aligned with Brazil and the BRICS+ system. ### Central America

Central America in 2050 is in the late stages of a profound reconfiguration driven by three forces: the collapse of the Monroe Doctrine after US federal dissolution removed the primary external obstacle to regional integration; the unification of Mexico with its northern Aztlán revolutionary zone creating a regional pole of attraction; and the climate-driven humanitarian pressure of the Dry Corridor drought generating existential pressure for political restructuring. The region's historical trajectory — five republics born from the 1823 Federal Republic of Central America, shattered by caudillo rivalries and US intervention — has come full circle. Without Washington's active suppression of unification movements (the 1907 Central American Court of Justice, the 1921 Federation attempt, the 1950s ODECA framework — all undermined by US opposition), the structural forces pulling the isthmus together are finally unconstrained.

**Central American Federation (CAF):** A revolutionary bloc anchored by the Northern Triangle core — Guatemala, El Salvador, and Honduras — operating as a confederal governance structure with a common external tariff, free movement, coordinated security policy, and a revived Central American Court of Justice. The CAF emerged in the 2040s as left-governments across the Northern Triangle, freed from US-backed security-force patronage networks, pursued integration as a survival strategy against climate-driven migration pressure and organized crime networks that no single state could contain. The CAF is not yet a full federation (Costa Rica and Panama remain outside) but functions as a deepening confederation with a trajectory toward reunification of the historical Central American space. The Northern Triangle alone accounts for ~35M of the region's ~55M population.

**Costa Rica:** The outlier — stable, democratic, ecotourism-driven, with no army and a strong institutional tradition. Costa Rica's caution toward the CAF reflects its historical exceptionalism (the "Switzerland of Central America") and its reluctance to absorb the governance deficits of its northern neighbors. Costa Rica maintains observer status and deep economic integration but preserves political autonomy. Its revolutionary character is measured and institutional — social-democratic stability rather than transformative upheaval.

**Panama:** The canal state — its entire economy and identity structured around the waterway. Panama's strategic calculus is unique: the canal's revenue stream (~$2.5B/yr pre-collapse) and the US security guarantee that protected it are both gone. In the post-American era, Panama navigates a complex triangle between the CAF (land border with Costa Rica, cultural and historical Central American identity), Colombia (land border, financial integration), and the BRICS+ powers that now dominate canal traffic (China is the second-largest user). Panama is the region's most pragmatic state — non-ideological, transactional, and cautious. It participates in CAF economic frameworks but maintains full sovereignty over canal operations and the Colón Free Zone.

**Belize:** The English-speaking Caribbean-identified state on the Central American mainland. Belize's orientation has always been Caribbean (CARICOM member, Commonwealth realm, English legal system, Afro-Caribbean cultural foundation) rather than Central American (Hispanic, Spanish-speaking, civil law). Its territorial dispute with Guatemala was resolved through CAF mediation in the 2040s — a demonstration of the CAF's institutional value. Belize maintains dual CARICOM-CAF membership as a bridge state, its sovereignty guaranteed by both regional blocs.

The CAF functions as a revolutionary front — not as ideologically coherent as CARICOM or the AES, but a genuine integration project in which member states pool sovereignty against climate and security pressures that none can face alone. Full Central American reunification (CA-6: Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama) is assessed as plausible by ~2075, contingent on whether the CAF's institutional deepening can absorb Costa Rica's caution and Panama's transactional independence.

### Africa

Africa in 2050 is shaped by two competing feedback loops that encompass every state on the continent. The **revolutionary loop** — exemplified by the EAF, the Federation of Sahel States (AES), the emerging Pan-Arab revolutionary current, and the Coastal West African Federation — pulls states toward deeper integration and eventual unification: shared institutional architecture, cultural-linguistic foundations, growing economic interdependence driven by the post-dollar BRICS+ financial system, and the removal of US/French external patronage that previously locked states in reactionary stasis. The **reactionary loop** generates mounting internal pressure — brittle regimes, climate stress, youth unemployment, external patron degradation — that forces states toward revolutionary flipping when pressure exceeds the state's capacity to contain it. The US collapse of the 2030s is the critical permissive condition: the external patron system that suppressed African revolutionary movements for decades is eliminated, producing a rubber-banding effect in which suppressed integration impulses accelerate. No state exists outside this dynamic — even apparent "holdouts" are states in the late stages of the reactionary track, where revolutionary potential is high but has not yet crossed the flip threshold.

**Federation of Sahel States (AES):** Revolutionary unification of six core states plus Northern Nigeria (~300M population) — Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Mauritania, Togo, and the northern states of Nigeria (19 states + FCT) forming a unified revolutionary confederation. The AES emerged from the collapse of French-backed regimes in the Sahel, the anti-French coup wave (Mali 2020, Burkina Faso 2022, Niger 2023), and the fragmentation of Nigeria. The federation's internal trajectory is not one of static authoritarianism but of a transitional revolutionary defensive posture — the military-junta phase is a self-liquidating emergency structure that replaces itself with revolutionary-socialist institutions as its conditions of origin dissolve.

The jihadi insurgencies (JNIM, ISGS, ISWAP) that provided the security rationale for military rule were sustained through a hybrid economy: local illicit networks — artisanal gold smuggling ($30B+/yr via UAE), livestock theft, kidnapping for ransom, and population taxation — combined with external weapons flows from Libya's post-Qaddafi chaos. The insurgency was not dependent on Western external funding (the "France funds jihadists" narrative used by Wagner/junta propaganda is unsupported by evidence) but on a gold smuggling pipeline that connected Sahelian conflict zones to UAE and Turkish refineries. This distinction is critical because it means the counter-insurgency strategy that succeeds is resource nationalization, not geopolitical realignment.

The AES juntas — particularly Traoré in Burkina Faso, Goïta in Mali, and Tchiani in Niger — have pursued an explicit resource-nationalization agenda that simultaneously undermines the insurgency economy and funds the revolutionary transition. Burkina Faso nationalized five gold mines (transferred to state-owned SOPAMIB), revoked foreign mining licenses, and demanded majority stakes in new projects — directly competing with the gold smuggling networks that funded JNIM/ISGS. Mali mandated 35% state ownership in mining (2023 code), seized the Yatela and Morila mines, and forced Barrick Gold into a $430M settlement. Niger cancelled gold concessions and revoked British oil licenses. As the state captures gold revenue that previously leaked to smuggling networks, the insurgency's funding pipeline degrades — not through military victory but through economic suffocation.

The AES's ideological foundation is the Sankara tradition — Thomas Sankara (Burkina Faso, 1983-87), a military captain who seized power via coup and implemented a revolutionary socialist program: mass vaccination, literacy (13% to 73% in four years), women's rights (FGM and polygamy banned), tree planting, IMF loan rejection, and self-sufficient development. Sankara was assassinated by the French-backed Compaoré coup after only four years — the revolution was cut short before it could consolidate. The current juntas explicitly claim Sankara as their ideological precedent and face structural advantages Sankara lacked: no French patron exists to back a counter-coup (France degraded, US collapsed), the BRICS+ financial system provides IMF-alternative lending, and gold nationalization funds the transition directly. The security emergency that justified centralized control naturally expires as the insurgency's funding dries up — the military phase is temporary by structural design, not by承诺.

The AES differs from the EAF's confederal model in its centralization — reflecting the Sankara tradition of centralized revolutionary governance and the N'Golo Diarra tradition of Sahelian empire-building — but the trajectory converges on the same revolutionary-socialist institutional deepening. The inclusion of Togo (Lomé as a Gulf of Guinea port) and Northern Nigeria (Kano as the economic anchor, the Sokoto Caliphate's historic political center) provides the Atlantic access and population base that makes the AES a continental-scale revolutionary pole. Niger remains the geographic and strategic heart — the AES's centroid. **→ See KML: Federation of Sahel States**

**Nigeria (rump):** The southern rump of the fragmented Nigerian state — 17 states and ~150M population, ranging from Lagos through the Niger Delta to the Cross River. A capitalist-democratic Atlantic state that retained the anglophone legal system, the Lagos financial sector, and the Niger Delta oil infrastructure after the northern states withdrew to join the AES. The Federation Account (oil revenue-sharing mechanism) collapsed when northern governors diverted their share to Abuja, triggering the constitutional crisis that produced the fragmentation. Coastal Nigeria retained the name "Nigeria" internationally and functions as a West African middle power — aligned with the Coastal West African Federation (Ghana-CIV axis) but independently governed from Lagos. The Niger Delta's oil production declined through the 2040s as global demand fell and onshore fields matured, but the sector remained viable for domestic consumption and regional petrochemicals. Lagos emerged as West Africa's financial hub following the AES withdrawal of northern banking institutions. Nigeria is a democratic-revolutionary state in the coastal West African tradition — capitalist, Atlantic-facing, multi-party — distinct from the AES's centralized Sahelian model. **→ See KML: Nigeria**

**Ghana:** The anchor of the Coastal West African Federation (Gold Coast Compact with Côte d'Ivoire). Ghana's stable democratic institutions and growing industrial base (automotive assembly, pharmaceuticals, digital services) made it the gravitational center of coastal West Africa post-Nigeria fragmentation. Accra functions as the coordination hub for the Ghana-CIV confederal security and monetary architecture. The CFA franc's dissolution following French monetary degradation removed a key dependency for the region. **→ See KML: Ghana**

**Côte d'Ivoire:** Confederal partner with Ghana under the Gold Coast Compact — coordinating monetary policy, external security, and trade across the Akan cultural axis. Abidjan retained its role as a Francophone West African economic hub, now operating outside the French monetary and security framework. The Ivorian cocoa-commodity economy transitioned toward processing and manufacturing, reducing raw-export dependency. The CIV-Ghana confederation is deep but falls short of unification — distinct legal systems and political traditions are preserved. **→ See KML: Côte d'Ivoire**

**Mano River Union (Mano Republic):** Full unification of Liberia and Sierra Leone (~12M population) following the US collapse and UK aid withdrawal. Liberia's US-client identity (including the dollar-based economy and American-Liberian elite structure) dissolved without Washington's backing, while Sierra Leone's post-conflict stability depended on UK budgetary support. The shared Mano River Basin geography, English language, and post-conflict institutional DNA (both emerging from devastating civil wars in the 1990s-2000s) provided the foundation for merger. The Mano Republic aligns with the Coastal West African Federation as a junior partner. Monrovia and Freetown are co-capitals. **→ See KML: Mano River Union**

**Senegambia:** Confederation of Senegal and The Gambia under a unified sovereignty framework — merging the Senegambia Confederation project that was abandoned in 1989. The Gambia's complete geographic enclavement within Senegal (a 450km riverine corridor entirely surrounded by Senegalese territory) makes unification structurally inevitable. The Wolof ethnic majority on both sides of the border and the shared Mouride/Sufi Islamic tradition facilitate cultural integration. The CFA franc dissolution (following French monetary withdrawal) removed the common currency obstacle. Dakar serves as the capital; Banjul retains administrative autonomy as the Gambian region's capital. **→ See KML: Senegambia**

**Benin:** Coastal West African state that navigated the post-French period by aligning with the AES neighbor (Togo) while maintaining independence. The Benin-Togo relationship is the closest inter-state relationship in coastal West Africa outside the Gold Coast Compact — shared Fon/Adja-Ewe cultural foundations, the Porto-Novo-Lomé economic corridor, and the historic Dahomey cultural space. Benin is not an AES member but operates as an associated state with deep cross-border integration, serving as a bridge between the AES and Coastal West African Federation. Cotonou functions as a secondary Atlantic port for the AES's Togolese access route. **→ See KML: Benin**

**Guinea:** Coastal West African state (Conakry) with a bifurcated geography — the coastal lowlands (the capital, the Bauxite-rich Boké region, the rice-producing Guinée Maritime) and the interior (Haute Guinée, the Fouta Djallon highlands, the Manding-speaking savannah). The interior is culturally Sahelian — Mandinka, Muslim, pastoral-agricultural — and gravitates toward the AES orbit. The coastal zone is distinct: Susu, coastal, mining-dependent, Atlantic-facing. This internal tension structures Guinea's post-French trajectory — too large and mineral-rich to be absorbed but internally divided between Sahelian and coastal orientations. Guinea remains independent as an AES-associated state, with the interior functionally integrated into the AES economic zone while Conakry maintains Atlantic sovereignty. The Simandou iron ore project (the world's largest untapped high-grade iron ore deposit) became operational in the late 2030s, underwritten by Chinese and BRICS+ investment — providing Guinea with independent revenue that sustains its balancing act between AES and coastal alignments. **→ See KML: Guinea**

**Guinea-Bissau:** Tiny Portuguese-speaking coastal state (~2M) whose post-colonial trajectory — a history of military coups, drug-trafficking state capture, and economic collapse — left it as the most vulnerable state in West Africa. After the US collapse and Portuguese/EU withdrawal, Guinea-Bissau's nominal sovereignty was preserved through Guinean (Conakry) protection — the two Guineas share the Bijagós cultural heritage and a deep history of Guinea-Bissau's liberation movement being based in Conakry. Guinea-Bissau functions as a de facto Guinean protectorate, serving as the Atlantic-facing buffer between the AES's Senegambian bloc and the coastal states. Bissau is effectively a district of Conakry's security and economic periphery. **→ See KML: Guinea-Bissau**

**Cabo Verde:** Atlantic island democracy (~600K population) that maintained its independent trajectory throughout the transformation. Cabo Verde is the revolutionary outlier of West Africa — a stable multi-party democracy, no army, a diaspora-driven economy (remittances exceed 10% of GDP), and a Creole culture (Crioulo) distinct from the mainland. Post-French, post-US collapse, Cabo Verde deepened its BRICS+ alignment (tourism from Brazil, Chinese infrastructure investment, Indian Ocean Commission observer status) while avoiding absorption into any mainland bloc. Its revolutionary character is democratic stability — the islands are a case study in sovereignty through smallness, not unification. **→ See KML: Cabo Verde**

**East African Federation (EAF):** Political confederation of 10 states (~380M population) — the most successful revolutionary integration project on the continent. The core members — Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, South Sudan, Somalia, Comoros, and the DRC (joined 2024) — are organized around a Swahili cultural foundation, shared institutional architecture, and growing intra-regional trade. The EAF deepened through the 2030s and 2040s, becoming the gravitational center of East and Central Africa. Its Somali member state expanded territorially in the 2040s: Somaliland was reabsorbed after the Berbera Axis collapsed (Israel-UAE patrons degraded), and the Ethiopian Somali Region unified with Somalia following Ethiopia's internal reconfiguration. The EAF's western reach now extends through the DRC to the Congo River — positioning the federation for Atlantic access via the Republic of Congo's Pointe-Noire port. Comoros' accession in the 2040s demonstrated the revolutionary feedback loop's culmination in unification: revolutionary states do not merely integrate, they merge. The DRC remains the federation's "problem member" — its resource wealth (70% of global cobalt, vast hydroelectric potential) underwrites the EAF's green energy leverage, but its governance deficits constrain its political weight. **→ See KML: East African Federation**

**Congo (Kinshasa):** The DRC is an EAF member state serving as the federation's western anchor. The transition from a failed extraction state to a member of a revolutionary federation was partial by 2050: the EAF's common security framework contained eastern militia activity (M23, ADF) more effectively than MONUSCO ever did, and the EAF Investment Bank financed infrastructure linking Kinshasa to the East African transport corridors. However, cobalt extraction remained characterized by exploitation, and the state barely functioned outside Kinshasa. The DRC is the EAF's longest-term transformation project — its membership is secure, its resource leverage is immense, but genuine revolutionary governance is a post-2050 horizon. **→ See KML: Congo (Kinshasa)**

**Ethiopia (rump):** A reduced Ethiopian state centered on Addis Ababa, having lost Tigray to independence and the Somali Region to unification with EAF-member Somalia. The GERD remains Ethiopia's defining sovereignty project — a revolutionary assertion of Nile headwaters control against Egypt's colonial-era water rights. The Berbera Axis (Israel-UAE-Ethiopia-Somaliland) collapsed as its patrons degraded; Ethiopia pivoted toward BRICS+/Quartet accommodation and EAF orbit. Internal ethnic federalism remains under tension — Oromia and Amhara are restive but remain within the state. Population ~140M (reduced from ~190M projection). **→ See KML: Ethiopia**

**Tigray:** Independent state emerging from the collapse of the 2022 Pretoria Agreement. Capital: Mekelle. The US collapse removed the diplomatic pressure that had sustained the ceasefire — as the Ethiopia-Eritrea-Tigray triangle fractured, Tigray reasserted sovereignty. Territorial disputes persist with Ethiopia and Amhara over the Western Tigray zones. Relations with Eritrea are complex — former allies in the 2020-22 war, now in an ambiguous strategic standoff. Tigray tilts toward the EAF orbit. Population ~7M. **→ See KML: Tigray**

**Eritrea:** Isolated authoritarian state held in reactionary stasis by the Isaias Afwerki regime. The US collapse and degradation of external patrons removed the last constraints on the regime's isolation — but also removed the external support (Egyptian basing, UAE engagement, Saudi financial backing) that sustained Eritrea's minimal economic function. The post-Isaias succession crisis is the most likely trigger for a revolutionary flip: the military-security elite has no succession mechanism, and a post-Isaias power struggle could open EAF alignment (shared Red Sea coast, Somali-speaking cross-border populations). Not yet in the revolutionary track by 2050 — but the reactionary pressure is terminal, and a flip within a decade is highly probable. **→ See KML: Eritrea**

**Djibouti:** Diminished strategic state whose entire economic model was based on being a great-power basing chokepoint. The US collapse (Camp Lemonnier closure), French degradation, and Ethiopia's pivot to alternative port arrangements destroyed Djibouti's revenue base. Residual Chinese PLAAF presence provides minimal fiscal support. Djibouti's Somali-speaking population and geographic position make it a natural EAF absorption target via Somalia — the Comoros precedent (small state absorbed by EAF for climate/security reasons) applies directly. Not yet a member by 2050, but the trajectory is clear: Djibouti's independence is not viable beyond the 2050s. **→ See KML: Djibouti**

**Mozambique:** The strongest candidate for EAF expansion beyond the current membership. The northern coast (Cabo Delgado, Nampula, Zambezia) is culturally part of the Swahili coast — Swahili is widely spoken as a trade language. The LNG sector collapsed as a stranded asset when global gas demand peaked in the 2030s, destroying the resource-extractive elite model. The Cabo Delgado insurgency, originally jihadist, transformed into a revolutionary movement aligned with the EAF. As the EAF consolidates "from the Congo Basin to the Somali Peninsula," Mozambique is a natural southward extension — providing continuous Indian Ocean coastline from Somalia to South Africa's border. EAF membership negotiations were underway by 2050 but not finalized. **→ See KML: Mozambique**

**Malawi:** Landlocked state whose port access is entirely dependent on EAF neighbors (Tanzania via Dar es Salaam, Mozambique via Beira and Nacala). When both neighbors are EAF-aligned, continued non-membership is structurally unsustainable. The Lake Malawi border with Tanzania is already settled. EAF membership is a question of timing, not choice — the Chewa-speaking population is not Swahili but the EAF has already accommodated non-Swahili members (DRC, South Sudan). Likely accession follows Mozambique's. **→ See KML: Malawi**

**Zambia:** Copper-producing landlocked state whose Copperbelt mining economy is structurally integrated with DRC's cobalt-copper sector across the shared border. The Dar es Salaam trade corridor (primary export route) is EAF-controlled. Zambia is not yet an EAF member by 2050, but economic integration pressure is overwhelming — the EAF Investment Bank finances cross-border infrastructure, the East African Shilling is the trade currency, and Zambia's copper is processed in DRC and Tanzanian smelters. Membership by the 2060s is highly probable. **→ See KML: Zambia**

**Zimbabwe:** Reactionary trap at terminal stage (Stage 4-5 degradation — post-Mugabe continued decline, dollarization crisis, informal economy dominant, elite capture complete). Unlike Mozambique or Zambia, Zimbabwe's orientation is southward, not eastward — Shona and Ndebele cultural identity, deep historical ties to South Africa (labor migration, ANC exile connections, shared liberation heritage). As South Africa completed its revolutionary reform in the 2040s, the millions-strong Zimbabwean diaspora in SA became vectors for revolutionary ideology flowing north. A post-Mnangagwa collapse is the likely trigger — Zimbabwe does not join the EAF but flips toward a South African-led Southern African revolutionary alignment. **→ See KML: Zimbabwe**

**Madagascar:** Large Indian Ocean island (~30M) whose Austronesian Malagasy language and culture place it outside the EAF's Swahili foundation. Climate vulnerability (cyclones, agricultural degradation) and the Comoros precedent create long-term pressure for EAF alignment, but cultural distance and geographic separation (400km Mozambique Channel) make membership a 2075+ prospect. By 2050, Madagascar remains independent with deepening economic ties to the EAF via Mozambique. **→ See KML: Madagascar**

**Mauritius:** Diversified small-island economy (finance, tourism, textiles) that navigated the post-US collapse through BRICS+ alignment. The Chagos Archipelago (ceded by the UK, including Diego Garcia, the former US base) provides strategic leverage — Mauritius monetizes Diego Garcia through basing rights with India and China. A BRICS+ financial node, not an EAF member — independence is viable and maintained. **→ See KML: Mauritius**

**Seychelles:** Tiny archipelago (~100K population). Climate vulnerability is existential but the EAF's Swahili foundation does not extend to the Seychellois Creole culture. Remains independent as a tourist-dependent microstate aligned with the Indian Ocean Commission and BRICS+. Too small for the revolutionary/reactionary binary to operate cleanly. **→ See KML: Seychelles**

**Angola:** Oil-dependent MPLA regime that entered terminal crisis following the dollar collapse and global oil demand decline. The MPLA's patronage network (the *sistema*) could not survive the disappearance of oil revenue — the party fractured in the 2030s. Angola became a contested zone between two revolutionary gravitational fields: the EAF pulling northward (Cabinda and northern Angola via the DRC) and revolutionary South Africa pulling southward (the oil-producing northern coast vs the southern savannah). Cabinda, the oil-rich exclave separated from mainland Angola by DRC territory, achieved de facto autonomy under EAF protection. The Angolan state did not fully collapse but transformed into a weak confederal entity with northern provinces leaning EAF and southern provinces leaning SA. **→ See KML: Angola**

**Cameroon:** French-backed unitary state that fragmented under the compound pressure of French patron collapse, Nigeria's fragmentation, and the unresolved Anglophone Crisis. Three outcomes by 2050: (1) The northern regions (Adamawa, North, Extreme North) — Muslim-majority, Sahel-aligned — joined the AES following Northern Nigeria's absorption into the federation. (2) Ambazonia (the Anglophone Northwest and Southwest Regions) achieved independence in the 2040s after French degradation removed Yaoundé's ability to hold the English-speaking cities (Bamenda, Buea). (3) Rump Cameroon — the Francophone south including Douala and Yaoundé — was pulled into the EAF's expanding westward reach, providing the EAF with Douala as a Gulf of Guinea port. Cameroon's fragmentation is the most consequential border change in Central Africa. **→ See KML: Cameroon**

**Central African Republic:** Russian-backed failed state whose patron (Russia, via Africa Corps/Wagner) withdrew as Russia declined into Chinese dependency. Without external support, Bangui could not hold the periphery — southern CAR (Bangui, the diamond and forest zones) was absorbed into the EAF through its DRC member, providing the EAF with a land bridge to the Chad/Sudan frontier. Northern CAR (Vakaga, Bamingui-Bangoran) became ungoverned territory contested between AES and EAF spheres. **→ See KML: Central African Republic**

**Chad:** Landlocked Sahelian state that became the primary contested zone between the AES and EAF gravitational fields. The Déby regime (a French-patronage dynasty since 1990) collapsed following French degradation and the US collapse — without external air power, intelligence, and financial support, the security forces fractured and the north slipped beyond state control. But Chad did not enter a "failed state" stasis: both the AES (expanding southward from its Niger-Mali core) and the EAF (pushing westward through CAR and Sudan) competed to fill the vacuum. The AES's cultural alignment with Chad's Sahelian Muslim identity, the chariot-warrior tradition of the Zaghawa military elite, and the shared Toubou networks across the Niger-Chad border create AES pull. The EAF's DRC-Sudan land bridge, the N'Djamena-Abéché corridor's economic ties to Sudan and CAR, and the Lake Chad Basin cross-border populations create EAF pull. By 2050, Chad was not absorbed into either bloc but existed as an active contestation zone — neither independent nor static, but the most pressured space between the Sahelian and East African revolutionary projects. The outcome will be resolved by 2060s. **→ See KML: Chad**

**Congo (Brazzaville):** Oil-dependent state whose Sassou Nguesso regime (in power since 1979) finally ended with his death in the 2030s. The post-Sassou regime faced an impossible strategic choice: the EAF already controlled the opposite bank of the Congo River (Kinshasa is an EAF member state), and the federation's need for Atlantic port access made Pointe-Noire an irresistible target. Brazzaville joined the EAF in the 2040s as a French-speaking member — the federation gained direct Atlantic access through Pointe-Noire, completing the EAF's transformation from an Indian Ocean power to a two-ocean revolutionary bloc. **→ See KML: Congo**

**Equatorial Guinea:** Obiang's oil kleptocracy (the most brutal and incompetent regime in Central Africa) collapsed after his death — oil production had fallen below 100K bpd by 2030. The island of Bioko (the capital Malabo) was absorbed by the EAF as a Zanzibar-style island territory. The mainland region (Río Muni) was partitioned between a diminished Cameroon (pre-fragmentation) and Gabon. **→ See KML: Equatorial Guinea**

**Gabon:** The most viable independent state of the Gulf of Guinea oil states — smaller population (~2.5M), more diversified economy (manganese for green energy, timber, hydroelectricity), and the 2023 coup at least broke the Bongo dynasty. Gabon retained independence as an EAF-associate state in 2050 — preserving internal autonomy while accepting EAF economic integration. Libreville functioned as a Gulf of Guinea financial hub. **→ See KML: Gabon**

**São Tomé and Príncipe:** Stable multiparty democracy that navigated the post-US-collapse world by aligning with the EAF as an observer state. The Nigeria Joint Development Zone (offshore oil rights) never became productive, but São Tomé's strategic Gulf of Guinea location made EAF alignment a natural choice for climate and security reasons — following the Comoros island precedent. Democratic institutions were preserved within the EAF framework. **→ See KML: São Tomé and Príncipe**

**Egypt:** Strained middle power in an advanced stage of the reactionary track. $169B external debt, chronic water stress, population 150M+ — held in reactionary stasis by US military aid ($1.3B/yr) and IMF/EU financial support. The US collapse removed these stabilizers, and the debt bomb exploded in the 2030s. The GERD reduced Nile flows by 10-25% — a fait accompli that Egypt struggled to adapt to. The Sisi regime's military-elite patronage network fractured under fiscal pressure. Egypt remains in the Quartet (Turkey-Saudi-Egypt-Pakistan) as a junior partner, but internal revolutionary potential is high — the same demographic and economic pressures that drove the 2011 uprising, now amplified by US patron removal. A revolutionary flip in the 2050s would align Egypt with Sudan along the Nile axis and position it as the anchor of a potential Pan-Arab revolutionary alignment. **→ See KML: Egypt**

**Libya:** De facto partition persisted through the period — Tripoli-based GNU (west) and Haftar's eastern administration (Benghazi, oil crescent) — under a veneer of de jure unity. Two central banks, two militaries, two foreign policy tracks. Multipolar competition (Turkey backing the west, Russia/UAE backing the east) sustained the fragmentation. Oil revenue-sharing mechanisms prevented collapse into renewed civil war but no reunification occurred. Libya is in a frozen reactionary state — both factions held in place by external patrons whose post-US-collapse degradation will force a resolution. Fezzan's southern zone became a smuggling corridor and Sahel spillover zone. Reunification under revolutionary terms — or a unified Libya entering a Pan-Arab framework — is the most likely post-2040s outcome. **→ See KML: Libya**

**Tunisia:** North African coastal state whose democratic backsliding (Kais Saied's 2021 self-coup) stabilized into a managed authoritarianism — but this was a reactionary interregnum, not a stable equilibrium. The democratic impulses of the 2011 revolution were suppressed, not extinguished. The US/EU patron withdrawal in the 2030s removed the external support (IMF loans, European tourism, security cooperation) that had sustained the managed-authoritarian model. Tunisia's educated population and civil society tradition reasserted themselves in the 2030s — a second revolutionary wave, this time without Western counter-revolution. Tunisia emerged from the reactionary track into an independent revolutionary state aligned with the emerging North African revolutionary current. **→ See KML: Tunisia**

**Algeria:** North African state whose "frozen" diplomatic conflict with Morocco over Western Sahara masked a deeper reactionary dynamic. The military-elite regime (le pouvoir) was sustained by hydrocarbon revenue and the external utility of the Western Sahara stalemate. The Hirak movement (2019-2020, suppressed by COVID and regime co-optation) re-emerged after the US collapse. Algeria's revolutionary potential is rooted in its anti-colonial founding (FLN, 1954-1962), its massive youth population, and the terminal decline of hydrocarbon revenue. The post-US-collapse Maghreb sees Algeria flip revolutionary, breaking the Western Sahara stalemate by transforming the conflict's terms — the Polisario front becomes a revolutionary ally rather than a frozen diplomatic card. **→ See KML: Algeria**

**Morocco:** Adaptive authoritarian monarchy that navigated the multipolar transition skillfully — deepening ties with China, Russia, Gulf states while maintaining Western economic links. The Western Sahara dispute was resolved in Morocco's favor (Sahara Autonomous Region under Moroccan sovereignty, US-diplomatic recognition, MINURSO phased down). But post-US-collapse, the diplomatic settlement was no longer backed by US enforcement. Morocco's vulnerability is structural: the monarchy's legitimacy depends on distribution capacity, and water stress (chronic, existential) plus declining Western investment erode that capacity. Morocco survived as a reactionary state through 2050 but faces mounting pressure from revolutionary Algeria to the east and the AES's expanded Sahel presence to the south. The monarchy's survival skill — adaptability — may produce a preemptive revolutionary turn, or it may hold as the last North African reactionary holdout. **→ See KML: Morocco**

**Sudan:** Post-civil-war rump state whose revolutionary potential was suppressed by US-backed counter-revolution (the 2019 revolution derailed by the 2021 coup). The Halaib Triangle was formally ceded to Egypt in exchange for reconstruction support; Bir Tawil incorporated into Sudanese territory. The US collapse removed the counter-revolutionary pressure — Sudan's revolutionary forces (the Forces of Freedom and Change, the resistance committees, the professional associations) re-emerged. By 2050, Sudan was in revolutionary transition, aligning with Egypt along the Nile axis. The Eastern Red Sea coast hosted Egyptian military basing, prefiguring a broader Nile-to-Red Sea revolutionary alignment. **→ See KML: Sudan**

### Asia

**China:** The world's largest economy and the single greatest beneficiary of the US collapse. A revolutionary state-directed model proven correct by events. Demographic crisis managed through automation at scale — humanoid robots offset 60%+ of the working-age shortfall. Taiwan's negotiated reunification (special administrative region with Hong Kong-level autonomy, ~2035-2038) provided nationalist reinforcement. The yuan is a major reserve currency in a multipolar financial system. **→ See KML: China** **→ See transition doc: [asia.md](../../2026-2050-transition/regions/asia.md#1-china--revolutionary-state-directed)**

**India:** A reactionary trap — RSS/BJP structural dominance, electoral autocracy, 40%+ graduate youth unemployment. Economic growth is capital-intensive and jobless — the demographic dividend squandered. Talent flight of secular and minority professionals accelerates. The democratic-revolutional model is the fading alternative. **→ See KML: India**

**Japan:** Slow-motion strategic erosion. Population declining at ~900K/year, 38% over 65, 1.9 workers per retiree. Auto industry decimated by Chinese EVs. Non-nuclear norm eroded gradually but Japan did not weaponize — it drifted into strategic irrelevance rather than crashed. A wealthy, aging middle power adjusting to Chinese regional hegemony. **→ See KML: Japan**

**Korea (Unified):** The ~40% scenario materialized — South Korea underwent a revolutionary transformation enabling reunification with the DPRK on terms of genuine parity. The unified Korean state (~90M population) inherits the DPRK's nuclear deterrent and Russia alliance, the ROK's industrial structure and technology, and China's guarantee. A self-reliant middle power with nuclear weapons and a coherent national identity. **→ See KML: Unified Korea**

**ASEAN:** The institutional model survived and thrived — collective neutrality, multipolar hedging, economic diversification without political alignment. Singapore became a BRICS+ financial node. Vietnam and Indonesia emerged strongest. The ASEAN bloc (~$4T combined GDP) is an indispensable middle in the Asian order. China dominates South China Sea security, but commercial access is maintained. **→ See KML: ASEAN**

### West Asia / Middle East

**Iran:** Nuclear-armed regional power. Tested a crude device in the mid-2030s, triggering a regional proliferation cascade. The IRGC consolidated power under Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei. The Axis of Resistance reconstituted as a looser network — less Iran-directed, more locally autonomous — but ideologically aligned and capable of coordinated asymmetric action. Iran controls Hormuz chokepoint leverage, though global oil demand decline gradually reduces its relevance. **→ See KML: Iran**

**Israel:** A nuclear-armed coastal rump state (~8,000-10,000 sq km) stretching from Haifa to Ashkelon. Territorial contraction completed through the 2030s-2040s — 700K+ West Bank settlers evacuated. The nuclear deterrent preserved the core but could not prevent the abandonment of occupied territory as the emigration-cascade, interceptor-gap, and manpower-crisis made settlements unholdable. Palestine exists as a contiguous sovereign state from Gaza through the West Bank to Jerusalem, with a corridor to Lebanon. **→ See KML: Israel** **→ See KML: Palestine**

**Saudi Arabia:** A wealthy but strategically constrained middle power past its oil peak. The GCC is split — the UAE chose the Israel axis (contracted with US collapse), Saudi Arabia aligned with the Quartet. Nuclear threshold state with latent capability achieved with Pakistani assistance. The absolute monarchy's legitimacy, dependent on distribution capacity, is under increasing strain as oil revenue declines. **→ See KML: Saudi Arabia**

**Quartet (Turkey-Saudi Arabia-Egypt-Pakistan):** Formalized as the post-NATO security architecture for West Asia. Turkey is the military-industrial anchor, Saudi Arabia provides capital, Egypt provides demographic weight and Suez leverage, Pakistan provides the nuclear deterrent. The defining institutional framework of post-American West Asia — containing Iran's Axis, managing Israel's contraction, and excluding US influence. **→ See KML: Quartet**

### Oceania

**Australia:** An independent middle power — the revolutionary pivot completed. AUKUS is dead (US submarines never arrived). Australia engaged BRICS+ as an observer, diversified trade to China and ASEAN, and built an independent defense posture. The transition from US client to multipolar actor was painful but complete by the 2040s. **→ See KML: Australia**

**New Zealand:** The quiet revolutionary model — independent foreign policy, nuclear-free since 1984, progressive governance, deepening Pacifica alignment. The US collapse changed nothing for New Zealand's strategic posture; it was already outside the US orbit. A stable, comfortable small-state model. **→ See KML: New Zealand**

**Pacific Islands:** "Blue Pacific" regionalism matured — the Pacific Islands Forum's 2050 Strategy delivered deepened regional governance, climate resilience financing, and collective diplomatic weight. Atoll states (Tuvalu, Kiribati, Marshall Islands) pioneered legal frameworks for sovereignty without habitable territory — their 200nm EEZs persist as sovereign zones even as physical land becomes marginal. **→ See KML: Pacific Islands Forum**

**US Pacific Territories:** Guam affiliated with the Pacifica/HFS under a compact with significant local Chamorro autonomy — the military bases (Andersen AFB, Naval Base Guam) are jointly managed assets. American Samoa absorbed into the Hawaiian Free State. CNMI aligned with Japan. The constitutional orphans of the US collapse found new homes. **→ See KML: Guam / HFS Compact**

### Polar Regions

**Arctic:** Governed through a pluralized framework — the Arctic Council with enhanced indigenous permanent participant status (Inuit Nunangat as a transnational political space). Russia, a reduced-capacity Canada, Nordic states, and indigenous nations manage the region cooperatively. The Northwest Passage is effectively international waters — Canada asserts sovereignty diplomatically but cannot enforce it militarily. Greenland achieved independence in principle (~2038-2042) with phased sovereignty over a decade. **→ See KML: Arctic Governance Zone**

**Antarctica:** The Antarctic Treaty System weakened but held — the Madrid Protocol mining ban was partially opened at the 2048 review. A "partial opening" regime permits mineral extraction in designated non-ice-covered zones with environmental safeguards and revenue-sharing. China operates the most substantial presence (Marie Byrd Land station, expanded logistics infrastructure). Argentina and Chile merged their Peninsula claims into a joint administration zone. The US Antarctic Program collapsed — no successor state inherited the role. **→ See KML: Antarctica**

### Orbital and Extraterrestrial

**Orbital Space:** Multipolar governance — China, Europe, and India as three recognized poles. A major debris collision in the 2030s forced international action: debris remediation missions, orbital traffic management, and a partial kinetic ASAT ban. The US orbital legacy (GPS, communications, science) fully degraded. Pacifica and NEC are space consumers dependent on Chinese, Indian, or European launch providers. The cislunar logistics layer (EML1 propellant depot, Queqiao constellation) is operational — China controls pricing for lunar-serviced traffic. **→ See KML: Orbital Governance Regime**

**Moon:** China's International Lunar Research Station (ILRS) is the only permanent crewed base — a south pole complex on the Shackleton-de Gerlache Connecting Ridge with operational water ice mining and propellant production. Five facility zones established: south pole research-mining-propellant complex, PSR robotic mining outposts, equatorial manufacturing hub (robotic), far side science zone (radio observatory), and cislunar logistics network. India and Europe maintain independent robotic access. The United States — which placed humans on the Moon in 1969 — is entirely absent by 2050. **→ See KML: Lunar Zones**

## Driving Forces

- **Economic collapse-driven fragmentation** — The 2026 dual shock (Iran War + AI bubble burst) destroyed federal fiscal capacity, making continued governance of the full US territory economically impossible **→ See transition doc: [borders.md Driver 1](../../2026-2050-transition/borders.md#driver-1-economic-collapse-driven-fragmentation)**
- **Revolutionary vs reactionary divergence** — Revolutionary states (deprivatization, state-directed investment, Landback incorporation) outperformed reactionary states (nationalist-capitalist/extractive models), creating a self-reinforcing demonstration effect **→ See transition doc: [borders.md Driver 6](../../2026-2050-transition/borders.md#driver-6-revolutionary-vs-reactionary-divergence)**
- **Indigenous sovereignty and Landback** — Federal collapse removed the enforcement mechanism for indigenous dispossession; indigenous nations emerged as independent actors with stronger governance capacity than many reactionary rumps **→ See transition doc: [borders.md Driver 7](../../2026-2050-transition/borders.md#driver-7-indigenous-sovereignty--landback)**
- **International order reconfiguration** — The BRICS+ alternative financial system replaced dollar dominance; the post-American multipolar order consolidated with no single hegemonic power **→ See transition doc: [borders.md Driver 5](../../2026-2050-transition/borders.md#driver-5-international-order-reconfiguration)**
- **West Asian axes formalization** — The Quartet and the Iranian-led Axis of Resistance formalized as the dual pillars of post-American West Asian security, with Israel's terminal contraction and Palestine's emergence as the defining territorial transformation **→ See transition doc: [regions/west-asia.md](../../2026-2050-transition/regions/west-asia.md)**

## Interactions With Other Domains

- **To Climate:** Border disputes over water resources in the Colorado River basin, the Nile (GERD), and the Tigris-Euphrates. Climate-driven migration has reshaped population distributions and citizenship across Africa, South Asia, and Europe. Successor states pursue divergent environmental policies — revolutionary states lead on decarbonization, reactionary states lag.
- **To Technology:** Technology governance regimes differ between successor states, creating regulatory fragmentation in AI, data, and biotechnology. The Pacifica and NEC maintain strong tech sectors; reactionary states have degraded digital infrastructure. Orbital space and cislunar infrastructure are dominated by non-US actors (China, Europe, India).
- **To Economy:** Trade blocs and economic zones align with political boundaries. Revolutionary states participate in BRICS+ financial systems; reactionary states are economically marginal. The GLPR's manufacturing base and Aztlán's semiconductor sector are integrated into BRICS+ supply chains.
- **To Demographics:** Borders define citizenship and migration regimes. Post-fragmentation settlement patterns show population concentration in revolutionary states (~58% of former US population). Climate migration from the Sahel, South Asia, and Pacific Islands has permanently altered regional demographics.
- **To Culture:** Political identities and ideological alignment map onto territorial boundaries. Revolutionary state identity is post-capitalist and internationalist; reactionary state identity is nationalist and defensive. Indigenous sovereignty has reshaped cultural narratives across North America.

## Key Uncertainties

- Potential for revolutionary/reactionary conflict to redraw borders further — New Afrika's consolidation and the reactionary Southern rumps' fragmentation are active zones
- Indigenous sovereignty recognition — expanding or contracting? The Navajo and Sequoyan precedents may inspire further assertions
- External power intervention in successor state dynamics — China maintains relationships with revolutionary states; reactionary states have no major external patrons
- Antarctica resource competition — the partial opening of the Madrid Protocol mining ban creates new pressures on the Antarctic Treaty system
- West Asian axes stability — whether the Quartet-Axis tug-of-war remains contained or escalates into direct confrontation
- Korean unification durability — whether the DPRK-ROK unified state maintains cohesion given the asymmetry of the two predecessor systems
- East Africa stability — whether Ethiopia's internal ethnic federalism holds (Oromia, Amhara tensions), whether Tigray's independence is durable or triggers further regional conflict, and whether the EAF can absorb its expanded Somali territory without internal destabilization
- Caribbean unification — whether CARICOM's deepening trajectory crosses the threshold into political unification by ~2075, or whether the Jamaica-Trinidad axis of reluctance and sovereignty attachments sustain the island-state model indefinitely
