## Key Changes From Previous Milestone

- The US dollar lost its global reserve currency status by 2030 (T-06), replaced by a multicurrency standard in which the yuan (~25%), euro (~20%), digital basket currency (~15%), and dollar (~15%) share reserve status, with remaining share distributed across regional currencies
- Post-US economic fragmentation produced ~19 successor economies across former US territory — revolutionary states (socialist-state-directed or social-democratic) collectively control ~65% of former US GDP, while reactionary states (nationalist-capitalist/extractive) experience stagnation or outright economic collapse
- Global economic center of gravity shifted decisively to Asia and the BRICS+ bloc — China is the world's largest economy (~$30T), the North Atlantic's share of global GDP fell below 25% for the first time since the Industrial Revolution, and BRICS+ members conduct ~60% of inter-member trade through alternative financial infrastructure
- Automation reached 40-60% penetration in manufacturing and 20-35% in services across advanced economies; revolutionary states have implemented forms of universal basic income or guaranteed employment, while reactionary states lack social safety nets, producing divergent labor market outcomes
- Post-capitalist economic models emerged as viable alternatives in revolutionary successor states and across parts of the Global South — the Pacifica formally declared a socialist economy by ~2044 (T-14), and the BRICS+ alternative financial system (operational since 2041, T-12) provides infrastructure for non-dollar-denominated economic activity

## Analysis

### Global Financial Architecture

The international financial system of 2050 bears little resemblance to the dollar-centric Bretton Woods II system that prevailed before 2026. The 2026 dual shock — the Iran War severing petrodollar recycling (T-01) and the AI bubble burst destroying US fiscal capacity (T-02) — shattered the mechanical foundation of dollar hegemony within a single year. By 2030 (T-06), formal reserve realignment was underway; by 2041 (T-12), the BRICS+ alternative financial system was operational and processing real transactions. The 2050 system is multipolar, fragmented along bloc lines, and in a state of competitive institutional development.

**BRICS+ alternative financial system:** Operational since May 2041 (T-12), the BRICS+ financial system is the defining infrastructure of the post-dollar global economy. Its components include:
- **BRICS+ Clearing House:** A multilateral settlement platform processing real-time cross-border payments in member currencies and the digital basket currency. Over 90 member and observer nations participate. Settlement times match or exceed SWIFT-era standards. The clearing house handles ~$4T in monthly transaction volume.
- **BRICS+ Multilateral Development Bank (MDB):** Capitalized at ~$500B equivalent, the MDB provides development finance, infrastructure lending, and crisis liquidity to member states. Headquarters in Shanghai. The MDB has surpassed the World Bank in annual lending volume since the mid-2040s. It operates on a one-member-one-vote governance model (weighted by GDP contribution for certain financial decisions).
- **BRICS+ Reserve Pool:** A collective reserve fund of ~$1.5T in pooled central bank reserves, available for balance-of-payments support and financial crisis response. Analogous to a BRICS+ IMF but with fewer conditionality requirements. The reserve pool has been activated three times since inception — for Argentina (2043), Turkey (2045), and Nigeria (2047).
- **Digital basket settlement platform:** A central bank digital currency (CBDC) infrastructure that enables instant settlement between member central banks. The basket is weighted: yuan (30%), euro (20%), digital basket currency unit or "BCU" (20%), gold (15%), yen and won (combined 10%), and regional currencies (5%). The BCU component is itself a managed basket — an algorithmic currency whose value is stabilized relative to a broad commodity index.

**Multicurrency reserve standard:** No single currency dominates global reserves in 2050. Central bank reserve composition is approximately:
- Yuan: ~25% — driven by China's economic weight, trade invoicing, and BRICS+ system integration
- Euro: ~20% — European Federation (unified single currency post-federalization)
- Digital basket currency (BCU): ~15% — growing rapidly as BRICS+ members shift reserves
- US dollar: ~15% — residual holdings in Americas trade and legacy contracts; declining gradually
- Yen, won, pound, and regional currencies: ~25% — reflecting the fragmentation of reserve preferences

The dollar's share continues its slow decline as legacy contracts expire and remaining dollar-denominated assets mature without full reinvestment. The US Treasury market — the deepest and most liquid in the world through the 2020s — has fragmented along with the US state, and no successor-issued bond has achieved comparable depth. The euro and yuan markets are the largest sovereign bond markets globally but neither provides the liquidity that Treasuries once did.

**Post-dollar trade settlement:** Commodity trade — energy, minerals, agricultural commodities — is predominantly settled in non-dollar currencies by 2050. The shift began with the Iran War in 2026, accelerated through the 2030s, and was formalized by the BRICS+ system launch in 2041. Trade patterns:
- BRICS+ internal trade: ~60% settled through BRICS+ clearing house in member currencies or BCU
- European Federation trade: Primarily euro-denominated
- China-Africa and China-Latin America trade: Increasingly yuan-denominated
- RCEP trade (Asia-Pacific): Mix of yuan, yen, won, and local currency pairs
- Americas trade: Residual dollar use, declining as successor states adopt own currencies

**Digital basket currency (BCU):** The BCU — a weighted basket of major economy currencies and commodities, operating as a CBDC for international settlement — is the most innovative component of the post-dollar system. Managed by the BRICS+ central bank consortium (headquartered in Beijing with rotating chair), the BCU is used primarily for:
- Central bank reserve holdings
- BRICS+ MDB lending and bond issuance
- Cross-border trade settlement between BRICS+ members
- Commodity contract pricing (energy, metals, agricultural)

The BCU is not used for domestic transactions in any member state — it functions purely as an international settlement and reserve instrument. Its value stability is maintained through the basket composition algorithm, which adjusts weights quarterly based on trade volumes, GDP shares, and commodity price indices.

**Capital flows:** Global capital markets are fragmented along bloc lines by 2050. Revolutionary successor states attract BRICS+ investment and development finance — the Pacifica, Atlantica, and Great Lakes have received significant BRICS+ MDB infrastructure lending. Reactionary states face capital flight and higher borrowing costs; their bonds trade at distressed levels, and international investment is limited to extractive sectors with high risk premiums. Texas and New Afrika, despite residual energy assets, face rapidly rising borrowing costs as global demand for fossil fuels declines. Cross-bloc capital flows are substantially reduced from pre-2026 levels — portfolio investment across blocs is minimal, foreign direct investment flows primarily within blocs, and sovereign wealth funds increasingly align reserve management with political alignment.

#### Trade Blocs

The global trading system has regionalized along political and currency lines. Five major trade blocs/systems operate in parallel, with limited cross-bloc trade.

**BRICS+ (Expanded):** The largest and most dynamic trade bloc by 2050.
- **Participants:** China, India, Brazil, Russia, South Africa (founding members); expanded membership includes Pacifica, Aztlán, Atlantica, Great Lakes, Indonesia, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Ethiopia, Nigeria, East African Federation, and 30+ observer states
- **Currencies:** Yuan, digital basket (BCU), local currency pairs. Member countries conduct ~60% of inter-member trade through the BRICS+ clearing house.
- **Key trade flows:** Manufactured goods (China, Great Lakes, Atlantica), energy and minerals (Russia, Saudi Arabia, Brazil, Aztlán), agricultural commodities (Brazil, India, EAF), technology and services (India, China, Pacifica, Atlantica), semiconductor components (Aztlán)
- **Integration depth:** The BRICS+ bloc is formally institutionalized — shared financial infrastructure (clearing house, MDB, reserve pool), a dispute resolution mechanism at the BRICS+ Trade Court (established 2043), and partial tariff harmonization across 50+ members and observers. Integration does not approach EU-level single-market depth, but it is operationally functional and deepening.

**European Federation:** The European Federation is a revolutionary federal state by 2050 — the culmination of an institutional revolutionary transformation of the EU framework. The former core/periphery monetary split was resolved through federalization; the Northern Euro became the EU's single currency and the periphery euro was absorbed. All former member states (now including Norway, Iceland, and Scotland) are administrative subdivisions of the federal European Federation.
- **Participants:** All former member states as administrative subdivisions, plus Norway, Iceland, and Scotland (acceded ~2035-2040s) — Nordic Council states (Finland, Sweden, Denmark, Norway, Iceland, Scotland), Benelux (Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg), France, Germany, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Greece, Austria, Ireland, Poland, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovenia, Croatia, Baltic states (Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania), Cyprus, Malta. Operating the unified Euro (EUR) with a single monetary policy, pooled debt issuance, and federal fiscal framework.
- **Key trade flows:** Advanced manufacturing and green tech (German industrial base, Eastern European manufacturing hubs), agricultural products and agribusiness (France, Spain, Italy, Poland — largest agricultural producer among revolutionary states), services and finance (Amsterdam, Frankfurt, Luxembourg, Paris), defense and aerospace (Airbus, Dassault, Thales), energy (North Sea, renewable exports from Nordics), tourism (Mediterranean — recovering from climate stress)
- **Integration depth:** Fully federalized — single market, unified currency, joint defense federation, coordinated industrial policy, common migration management, unrestricted labor mobility, and federal fiscal transfers. The EU is the most integrated revolutionary state in the world by 2050.

**Asian Supply Chains:** China-centered manufacturing network adapting to post-globalization conditions.
- **Participants:** China, Japan, ROK, DPRK, Southeast Asian Federation (SEAF — Vietnam, Indonesia, Thailand, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, plus Brunei, Cambodia, East Timor, Laos, Myanmar), Australia (observer within BRICS+), Taiwan (as a special administrative region of China since ~2035-2038)
- **Currencies:** Yuan, yen, won, SEAF currencies (SGD as anchor, local currencies). RCEP framework governs cross-border trade terms.
- **Key trade flows:** Advanced manufacturing and electronics (China, Korea, SEAF), energy and resources (Australia, Indonesia, Malaysia — LNG, minerals), agricultural products (Thailand, Vietnam), financial services (Singapore as SEAF BRICS+ financial node)
- **Integration depth:** RCEP provides a tariff framework and rules of origin. Automation has reshaped the labor-cost advantage that drove pre-2026 Asian supply chains — China's humanoid robot production (~200K units/year) and Indonesia's automation investments mean that manufacturing location decisions are driven by energy costs, infrastructure quality, and proximity to end markets rather than labor costs.

**Americas Trade:** Residual dollar zone in the Americas, declining in importance.
- **Participants:** Post-Canadian successor states (Canadian rump, Quebec Republic, Maritime Republic, Newfoundland, Manitoba, Inuit Nunangat, Denendeh), Mexico (unified, BRICS+ affiliated), Caribbean states, residual reactionary successor states (Texas, Florida, Heartland — shrinking), plus revolutionary successor states (New Afrika, Pacifica, Aztlán) that now trade more with BRICS+ and Asia than with Americas neighbors
- **Currencies:** Canadian dollar (rump), Quebec Franc, Maritime Dollar, Newfoundland Dollar, Manitoba Dollar, Mexican peso, successor state currencies (Pacific Dollar, Atlantic Dollar, Great Lakes Dollar, Aztlán Peso, Deseret Dollar), residual USD in reactionary territories
- **Key trade flows:** Energy (Canadian rump oil/gas — declining, but continues to EU and Atlantica), manufactured goods (Great Lakes, Atlantica), agricultural (Heartland, Canadian rump, Manitoba, Mexico), hydroelectric exports (Quebec Republic, Manitoba, Newfoundland to Atlantica), semiconductor components (CSR to BRICS+)
- **Integration depth:** USMCA frameworks collapsed with US dissolution. Successor states maintain bilateral trade agreements — Pacifica-Atlantica trade compact, Aztlán-Mexico energy agreement, Great Lakes-Canadian rump Great Lakes shipping treaty (contested), Maritime Republic-Atlantica free movement zone, Quebec Republic-Newfoundland no-trade status (Labrador dispute). There is no single Americas trade framework.

**African Blocs:** The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is operational but unevenly implemented.
- **Participants:** 54 of 55 African Union member states (Eritrea remains outside). Regional blocs (ECOWAS, SADC, EAC) remain relevant as implementation sub-units.
- **Key trade flows:** Agricultural products (intra-African), minerals and critical resources (lithium, cobalt, rare earths — to BRICS+ and European markets), manufactured goods (limited — EAF and South Africa), energy (East African geothermal, Nigerian and Angolan oil/gas — declining)
- **Integration depth:** AfCFTA has eliminated tariffs on 90% of goods but faces non-tariff barriers, infrastructure gaps, and limited manufacturing capacity outside a few economies. The East African Federation (EAF) is the most integrated sub-region — functioning as a confederation with a common currency (East African Shilling) and coordinated economic policy. The Confederation of Sahel States (AES) operates a separate common currency. ECOWAS remains fragmented on monetary union. SADC maintains a free trade area with uneven compliance.

### Labor & Automation

The 2050 employment landscape is defined by the uneven penetration of automation, divergent policy responses, and the first generation of "post-work" economic experiments.

**Automation penetration by sector (advanced economies — revolutionary states, European Federation, China, Japan, ROK, DPRK):**
- Manufacturing: 40-60% of tasks automated. The Great Lakes' manufacturing anchor operates at ~50% automation (by task count), with human workers concentrated in supervision, maintenance, quality assurance, and specialized fabrication. China's manufacturing sector leads globally at ~65-70% automation.
- Logistics and transportation: 50-70% automated. Autonomous trucking and last-mile delivery are standard in revolutionary states and the European Federation. Port operations are near-fully automated. Human roles persist in oversight, exception handling, and local delivery in areas with degraded infrastructure.
- Retail: 30-40% automated. Automated checkout, inventory management, and warehouse operations. Human roles persist in customer service, specialized retail, and community markets.
- Financial services: 40-50% automated. Algorithmic trading, automated underwriting, AI-driven financial advice, and fraud detection. Human roles in relationship management, regulatory compliance, and strategic decision-making.
- Healthcare: 15-25% automated. Diagnostic AI, robotic surgery assistance, automated administration. Human roles dominate in direct patient care, complex diagnosis, and therapeutic relationships.
- Agriculture: 20-30% automated in advanced economies. Autonomous tractors, drone-based monitoring, automated harvesting for row crops. Labor-intensive in specialty crops and reactionary state agriculture.

**Automation penetration in reactionary states:** Significantly lower — 10-25% across sectors, driven by degraded infrastructure, lack of capital for automation investment, and reliance on low-wage labor. Kentucky's and Tennessee's extraction and manufacturing remnants are the most labor-intensive sectors in the former US.

→ See technology snapshot: [AI & Computation](../../2050-snapshot/domains/technology.md#ai--computation) for automation infrastructure details.

**UBI / workfare programs:** The successor state landscape is divided between revolutionary states that have implemented forms of universal basic income or guaranteed employment and reactionary states that largely lack social safety nets:
- **Pacifica:** Universal Basic Income established 2039 (the Pacific Dividend), funded by a combination of automation taxes, carbon pricing revenue, and public enterprise dividends. Provides ~$12K/year equivalent per adult citizen, indexed to productivity growth. Supplemented by universal healthcare (Pacifica Unified Health Service, 2039) and free higher education.
- **Atlantica:** Guaranteed employment program (Atlantic Works, established 2041) — the state guarantees a job at a living wage for any citizen willing to work. Supplemented by unemployment insurance and retraining benefits. The jobs program functions as an automatic stabilizer and maintains labor force attachment.
- **Great Lakes:** Hybrid — universal basic income for citizens unable to work (disability, elderly, caregivers) plus guaranteed employment in manufacturing and infrastructure sectors for able-bodied adults. The Great Lakes' reindustrialization program intentionally maintains high employment; the "right to a productive role" is part of the republic's founding social contract.
- **Aztlán:** Universal basic services (healthcare, education, housing, water, internet) rather than cash payments. Employment guarantee in renewable energy, semiconductor, and indigenous cooperative sectors. Aztlán's model emphasizes collective provision over individual cash transfers.
- **European Core:** Universal basic income implemented in Finland (pilot expanded nationally, 2034), Netherlands (2038), and Denmark (2040). Other core states maintain strong social-democratic welfare states with automation-adjusted unemployment insurance.
- **China:** Social credit system extended to include employment guarantees and automation dividends. State-directed job placement and retraining for displaced workers. The demographic decline provides some structural offset — fewer workers entering the labor force reduces the scale of the automation-displacement problem.
- **Reactionary states:** No universal income or employment programs. Social safety nets that existed during the US federal period collapsed with the federal government. Charitable and religious organizations (LDS Church in Deseret) provide residual support. Energy workers across the former Gulf region who lost jobs to automation have no income replacement; labor organizing in the petrochemical sector by ~2043-2045 was partly a response to this gap.

**Labor migration between entities:** Post-2050 migration patterns are shaped by economic opportunity, climate drivers, and political alignment:
- Skilled labor flows toward revolutionary states and BRICS+ economies — the Pacifica, Atlantica, and European Federation are net recipients of educated professionals from reactionary states, India (talent flight from electoral authoritarian conditions), and climate-affected regions
- Reactionary states experience brain drain — young, educated workers leave for revolutionary states and BRICS+ economies; the Deseret's youth exodus is demographically significant
- Climate migrants fill labor gaps in some destination regions — the Pacifica and Atlantica absorb climate migrants from Central America and the Caribbean into expanding care, construction, and service sectors
- Cross-bloc labor mobility is limited — revolutionary states impose immigration controls, reactionary states lose population but do not attract in-migration

**Post-work question:** In highly automated economies (Pacifica, Atlantica, European Federation, China), the relationship between employment and livelihood is being renegotiated:
- Four-day workweeks are standard in the Pacifica (legislated 2023 pilot expanded and codified, 32-hour workweek by 2038). Atlantica and European Federation have implemented 35-hour workweek standards. Great Lakes maintains a 36-hour workweek with flexibility.
- Reduced working hours and expanded leisure time are emerging norms in automated sectors — the Pacifica's cultural shift from productivity-oriented to well-being-oriented work culture is most advanced.
- "Post-work" debates are active but not resolved — the Pacifica and European Federation frame universal basic income as a citizenship right rather than a welfare program; reactionary states frame work as a moral obligation and reject income guarantees.
- The challenge of purpose and meaning in a highly automated society is a defining cultural question in revolutionary states — see culture snapshot for ideological dimensions.

### Economic Profiles — US Successor States

Entity-by-entity economic profiles covering all 19 successor states. Each profile includes GDP range, dominant sectors, trade partners and bloc alignment, economic model, currency status, and labor market character.

#### Revolutionary States

**Pacifica:**
- **GDP:** ~$2.5T
- **Dominant sectors:** Technology (Silicon Valley legacy, AI, software, cloud), green energy (solar, wind, geothermal — best renewable endowment in former US), entertainment (Hollywood and streaming), specialty agriculture (Central Valley, wine country), manufacturing (semiconductor design, aerospace, advanced materials), gaming/tourism (Las Vegas/Clark County)
- **Trade partners and bloc alignment:** BRICS+ (primary), Atlantica, Aztlán, European Federation, Canadian rump, East Asia. The Pacifica is a BRICS+ member and a founding participant in the BRICS+ clearing house and MDB.
- **Economic model:** Socialist-state-directed with strong market elements in small and medium enterprise. Major industries (energy, transportation, healthcare, housing) are publicly owned or regulated as public utilities. Strategic industries operate under state coordination; SME sector operates with market competition within a regulatory framework.
- **Currency:** Pacific Dollar (PAD) — floated, managed by the Pacific Reserve Bank. PAD is convertible to digital basket (BCU) and yuan. Trade-weighted exchange rate policy.
- **Labor market character:** Highly automated (technology, manufacturing, logistics) with UBI floor. Service sector mixed (automated + human). Education and healthcare remain labor-intensive. ~55M population.

**New Afrika:**
- **GDP:** ~$400B (post-revolutionary reconstruction underway — down significantly from pre-CSA baseline)
- **Dominant sectors:** Gulf shipping (New Orleans, Mobile, Savannah, Charleston — trade volume recovering as revolutionary ties strengthen with Great Lakes and Atlantica), agriculture (cotton, rice, soybeans — reorganizing under cooperatives), manufacturing (reconstituting under public ownership — GA $69B, AL $35B, SC $35B, LA $45B legacy output, most disrupted)

The "common prosperity" framework has reduced inequality from its 2020s peak. The demographic crisis (population has declined from 1.41B to ~1.25B) is managed through automation — humanoid robots offset 60%+ of the working-age population decline in manufacturing, logistics, and elder care.

The working-age population has declined by ~150M since the 2020s, but automation has offset most of the labor force loss. The "post-work" question is actively managed — the state provides automation dividends, retraining programs, and guaranteed employment in public services. Skilled workers (AI engineers, roboticists, renewable energy technicians) are in high demand. The traditional manufacturing workforce (the "world's factory" generation) has been largely retrained or retired. Rural-to-urban migration has reversed in some regions as automation reduces urban labor demand. The service sector (elder care, healthcare, education) absorbs workers displaced from automated manufacturing.

Economic diversification beyond mining and energy is limited — the small population (~3.4M) means niche industries (cashmere, organic meat, eco-tourism) can be nationally significant. Ulaanbaatar's ger districts (informal settlements housing ~60% of the capital's population) remain an unresolved urbanization challenge — air pollution from coal stoves has been largely eliminated by the electrification and renewable transition.
- **Currency:** Tögrög (MNT) — managed float, partially convertible. Trade with China increasingly settled in yuan. The tögrög is not internationally traded but is stable within its managed band.
- **Labor market character:** Mixed. Renewable energy sector capital-intensive (automated solar/wind farms employ relatively few workers). Mining capital-intensive with skilled technical workforce. Agriculture and pastoralism labor-intensive — ~25% of workforce, declining as urbanization continues. Youth unemployment is moderate (~12%) — the small population limits labor surplus. Skilled workers increasingly migrate to China and the Pacifica for higher wages. The renewable energy transition has created a stable fiscal foundation but not mass employment — the "resource curse" pattern of capital-intensive extraction with limited job creation persists even with green energy. ~3.4M population.

**ROK (Republic of Korea, formerly South Korea):**
- **GDP:** ~$1.6T (declining — demographic collapse and loss of US market access offset by residual industrial strength)
- **Dominant sectors:** Advanced manufacturing (semiconductors — Samsung, SK Hynix retain global competitiveness in memory chips and logic; automotive — Hyundai/Kia, declining from Chinese EV competition but maintaining niche in commercial vehicles and defense manufacturing), shipbuilding (heavy industry — LNG carriers, container ships, naval vessels — still globally competitive), technology services (AI, robotics, biotech — R&D-intensive but market reach constrained by Chinese tech ecosystem dominance), defense (arms exports — K9 howitzers, K2 tanks, FA-50 jets — growing export market to BRICS+ and non-aligned states replacing Russian and US arms), entertainment and culture (K-continuation — streaming content, gaming, K-pop evolved for post-American market)
- **Trade partners and bloc alignment:** China (primary — largest trade partner by a wide margin, semiconductor and electronics exports, industrial goods), ASEAN (supply chains, RCEP framework), European Federation (automotive and electronics), Pacifica (technology trade). The US collapse removed the ROK's primary security guarantor and a significant export market — ROK has reoriented toward Chinese-dominated supply chains. Alignment: reactionary — the ROK retains its US-legacy alliance structures in institutional form but the alliance is functionally obsolete; China is the de facto security and economic hegemon.
- **Economic model:** Reactionary degradation — the ROK economy is structurally trapped. TFR of 0.72 (world's lowest) means the domestic consumer base is evaporating and the workforce is imploding. Household debt exceeding 200% of disposable income constrains domestic demand. The export-oriented chaebol model (Samsung, Hyundai, SK, LG) was built for a US-dominated global economy that no longer exists. The chaebols have adapted — Samsung and SK Hynix supply semiconductors to Chinese tech firms, Hyundai sells EVs to ASEAN and BRICS+ markets — but they operate from a position of declining leverage. The economic trajectory is managed decline: productivity gains from automation offset some labor force losses, and the industrial structure retains world-class competitiveness in niche sectors, but the structural conditions (demographics, debt, market access) make GDP growth impossible.
- **Currency:** Won (KRW) — freely floating, internationally traded. The won has depreciated significantly against the yuan since the US collapse — a managed decline reflecting reduced export competitiveness and demographic headwinds. Won-yuan swap line established with PBOC for trade settlement.
- **Labor market character:** Highly automated. Manufacturing 55%+ automated — semiconductor fabrication is near-fully automated. The working-age population has collapsed from ~37M (2020s) to ~22M — one of the most dramatic labor force contractions in human history. Immigration (ASEAN, South Asia) partially offsets decline but strict immigration policies (legacy of ethnic homogeneity preference) limit the offset. The labor shortage is acute in elder care, services, construction, and agriculture — sectors where automation is difficult. The dual labor market (chaebol regular workers vs. irregular/precarious workers) persists. The social safety net has been expanded under the Lee Jae-myung engagement path — universal basic services (healthcare, education, elder care) funded by debt and declining but still substantial industrial revenue.

**DPRK (Democratic People's Republic of Korea, formerly North Korea):**
- **GDP:** ~$120B (growing rapidly from a very low base — sanctions removal and Russia alliance unlock pent-up growth potential; nominal GDP understates real economic activity due to sanctioned-economy measurement challenges)
- **Dominant sectors:** Heavy industry and manufacturing (steel — Kim Chaek Iron and Steel Complex modernized with Russian technology, machinery, chemicals), military-industrial (weapons exports — artillery shells, missiles, small arms to Russia, Iran, and BRICS+ states; ballistic missile and nuclear technology — codified deterrent, limited export), mining and minerals (coal — anthracite exports to China; rare earths, magnesite, zinc — growing extraction sector with Russian and Chinese investment), energy (hydroelectric — infrastructure expanded with Russian cooperation; nuclear energy — experimental light-water reactor at Yongbyon repurposed for civilian power with Russian assistance), agriculture (collectivized — rice, maize, potatoes; chronic food insecurity resolved through trade access and agricultural modernization investment post-sanctions), services (nascent — Kaesong industrial zone expanded into DPRK's first special economic zone, modeled on Chinese SEZs of the 1980s-1990s)
- **Trade partners and bloc alignment:** Russia (primary — CSPT alliance, weapons exports, energy imports, technology transfer), China (trade, investment, security guarantee — the traditional patron relationship has evolved toward partnership as DPRK's strategic autonomy increased), Iran (arms cooperation, anti-Western alignment). The DPRK is not a BRICS+ member but trades primarily through BRICS+ infrastructure and has observer status. Alignment: revolutionary ascendancy — the DPRK is the most dramatic beneficiary of the US collapse. Per D-03, the UN sanctions regime that strangled its economy for three decades vanished with US withdrawal from the UN Security Council veto system (the multipolar UN reform of the 2030s). The CSPT with Russia (June 2024) provided the military and economic partnership that sanctions had denied.
- **Economic model:** Revolutionary state-directed — the DPRK operates a Juche-inspired (self-reliance) command economy that has been selectively opened to external trade and investment. The 20×10 plan (20 factories per province per 10 years, initiated in the 2020s) has been expanded with Russian technology and Chinese investment. Special Economic Zones (SEZs) — Kaesong, Rason, Sinuiju — operate under separate legal frameworks allowing foreign investment, profit repatriation, and market-based wage systems. The domestic economy remains state-controlled in heavy industry, energy, and strategic sectors. Agriculture has been partially decollectivized — the "field responsibility system" (analogous to China's household responsibility system of the 1980s) has improved food production. The economic transformation is real but uneven — Pyongyang and the SEZs are modernizing rapidly; the interior and northeastern provinces lag significantly.
- **Currency:** DPRK won (KPW) — non-convertible, state-managed. Foreign trade and SEZ transactions use yuan, ruble, and BCU. The DPRK won functions as a domestic currency only. A parallel foreign exchange certificate system (similar to China's FEC of the 1980s) operates in SEZs and for foreign trade.
- **Labor market character:** State-mobilized, transitioning. The workforce (~15M) is primarily employed through state allocation — the socialist labor system remains the default. SEZs and foreign-invested enterprises operate with market-based hiring and wages, creating a two-tier labor market. The DPRK's labor advantage is its disciplined, literate workforce with low wages — attracting manufacturing investment from China (relocating labor-intensive production from increasingly automated Chinese factories) and Russia (processing industries). The "army-first" (Songun) policy has been partially demobilized as military personnel are redeployed to economic construction — the Korean People's Army operates construction brigades and industrial units. The demographic profile is favorable compared to the ROK — TFR of ~1.9, younger median age (~34), and a population that has not experienced the demographic collapse of its southern counterpart.

- **Currency:** Australian dollar (AUD) — convertible to BCU; actively traded in Asia-Pacific markets; not a reserve currency
- **Labor market:** Highly automated in mining and agriculture (autonomous trucks, drones); services mixed; managed migration programs (Pacific Engagement Visa, skilled migration) fill labor gaps; climate-driven internal migration from heat-stressed inland regions ongoing

**New Zealand:**

**Papua New Guinea:** Resource wealth without development — the resource curse in practice.

## Driving Forces

The 2050 economic landscape is the end-state of trajectories whose key drivers are documented in the 2026-2049 transition analysis:

- **Dollar hegemony collapse and multicurrency emergence** — The 2026 dual shock (Iran War severing petrodollar recycling + AI bubble burst destroying US fiscal capacity) shattered the dollar's reserve status within five years, producing the multicurrency reserve standard and BRICS+ alternative financial system that define 2050's financial architecture **→ See transition doc: [economy.md Driver 1](../../2026-2050-transition/economy.md#driver-1-dollar-hegemony-collapse), events T-01, T-02, T-06, T-12**
- **Post-US economic fragmentation** — The collapse of federal fiscal capacity after the 2026 dual shock forced states to manage their own economies, producing 19 successor entities with divergent economic models — revolutionary states (socialist-state-directed) outperforming reactionary states (nationalist-capitalist/extractive) across all metrics **→ See transition doc: [economy.md Driver 2](../../2026-2050-transition/economy.md#driver-2-post-us-economic-fragmentation), events T-11, T-14**
- **Automation and AI-driven economic transformation** — The post-bubble acceleration of efficiency-driven AI deployment restructured labor markets across advanced economies, creating the automation penetration levels and the "post-work" policy question that defines revolutionary state labor policy **→ See transition doc: [economy.md Driver 5](../../2026-2050-transition/economy.md#driver-5-automation-driven-labor-restructuring), event T-02**
- **BRICS+ alternative financial system development** — The operational launch in 2041 (T-12) provided non-dollar financial infrastructure for revolutionary states, the Global South, and BRICS+ members, creating the institutional foundation for the post-dollar economic order **→ See transition doc: [economy.md Driver 1](../../2026-2050-transition/economy.md#driver-1-dollar-hegemony-collapse), event T-12**
- **Climate economics — damages, adaptation costs, and green transition** — The economic transformation driven by climate damages (Amazon dieback T-15, water crises, agricultural zone shifts) and the green energy transition accelerated renewable deployment in revolutionary states while stranding fossil fuel assets in reactionary states **→ See transition doc: [economy.md Driver 4](../../2026-2050-transition/economy.md#driver-4-global-supply-chain-reconfiguration), event T-15**

## Interactions With Other Domains

- **To Borders:** Economic blocs align with political boundaries — BRICS+ member states share financial infrastructure, tariff frameworks, and currency arrangements that reinforce bloc cohesion. Successor state economic divergence (revolutionary vs reactionary) drives territorial dynamics and hardens borders. The Great Lakes' manufacturing anchor, the Aztlán's semiconductor and solar energy base, and the Pacifica's technology sector create economic gravity that pulls border alignments. **→ See also: [borders-geopolitics.md](../../2050-snapshot/domains/borders-geopolitics.md)**
- **To Technology:** Automation penetration determines labor market character — the technology snapshot's AI governance models (regulatory, precautionary, permissive) directly shape which economic sectors automate and how fast. Energy systems (renewable penetration, grid architecture) determine economic model viability for successor states — the Aztlán's solar export economy would be impossible without the HVDC infrastructure and storage technology described in the technology snapshot. AI-driven economic planning in revolutionary states depends on the computational infrastructure described in the technology domain. **→ See also: [technology.md](../../2050-snapshot/domains/technology.md)**
- **To Demographics:** Labor migration responds to economic opportunity — revolutionary states with UBI, guaranteed employment, and automated economies attract skilled labor from reactionary states and the Global South. Aging populations strain public finances in all advanced economies (European Federation, China, Japan, ROK, DPRK), while automation offsets labor force decline in revolutionary states. The demographic dividend in Africa (EAF, Nigeria) and parts of Asia (India, ASEAN) interacts with their labor-intensive economic structures. **→ See also: demographics.md**
- **To Climate:** Economic damages from climate change vary dramatically by region — Amazon dieback (T-15) reduced Brazil's agricultural output by 30-60%; water crises constrain economic growth in Aztlán, the American West, and the Heartland; agricultural zone shifts restructure rural economies globally. Adaptation costs strain fiscal capacity in reactionary states while revolutionary states invest in climate-resilient infrastructure. The green energy transition is both an economic opportunity (renewable manufacturing, EV production) and a disruption (stranded fossil fuel assets, declining extraction employment). **→ See also: [climate.md](../../2050-snapshot/domains/climate.md)**
- **To Culture:** Post-capitalist ideology shapes economic model choice — revolutionary states' socialist economic systems are expressions of the ideological shift described in the culture snapshot. Consumer culture fragments along bloc lines — revolutionary state citizens participate in regulated, sustainability-oriented consumer economies; reactionary state citizens experience degraded, scarcity-constrained consumption. Work/leisure norms diverge — the four-day workweek and post-work debates in revolutionary states contrast with survival-driven labor in reactionary entities. Economic anxiety and precarity in reactionary states fuel right-wing cultural radicalization. **→ See also: culture.md**

## Key Uncertainties

- **Revolutionary state economic sustainability:** Whether socialist-state-directed economies in Pacifica, Great Lakes, Aztlán, FRSR, and HFS can sustain growth and productivity improvements over the long term, or whether state-directed allocation reproduces historic inefficiencies. The BRICS+ trade integration reduces autarky risk, but internal governance quality, innovation incentives, and capital allocation efficiency remain untested at scale. The Pacifica's hybrid model (state-directed strategic industries + market SME sector) is the key experiment — its success or failure will shape the trajectory for other revolutionary states.
- **Reactionary state economic transition:** Whether reactionary states can transition from extractive/nationalist-capitalist models before resource depletion and capital flight cause total economic collapse. New Afrika's post-revolutionary reconstruction (underway in 2050) will determine whether the Gulf stabilizes as a socialist economy or fragments further. Texas's energy-collapsed economy faces existential decline. The Deseret's theocratic-capitalist model is culturally intact but economically hollow — whether it degrades slowly or collapses is an open question.
- **BRICS+ financial system stability:** The BRICS+ clearing house, MDB, reserve pool, and BCU are operational and processing real transactions, but internal tensions — China-India rivalry, competing currency interests, divergent economic governance models — could undermine system cohesion. A financial crisis in a major BRICS+ economy (China's real estate legacy, India's jobless growth, Brazil's climate shock) could test the reserve pool and solidarity mechanisms beyond their design capacity.
- **Automation trajectory and distribution:** Faster automation adoption could exacerbate inequality or accelerate post-work transition depending on policy response. Revolutionary states have implemented UBI or guaranteed employment; if automation accelerates beyond what these programs can absorb (e.g., AI displacing creative and professional services that were assumed automation-resistant), even the Pacifica and Atlantica models could face fiscal and social strain. If automation slows (energy costs, regulatory barriers, social resistance), the labor-intensive economies of reactionary states may stabilize, reducing the economic divergence driving migration.
- **Climate economic feedback:** Climate economic damages could accelerate beyond current projections — cascading crop failures, more frequent extreme weather events, or AMOC disruption would fundamentally reshape all economic assumptions. The green energy transition's pace (already complete in revolutionary states, lagging in reactionary territories) determines both remaining emissions and the competitiveness of renewable-powered economies relative to fossil-dependent ones. Aztlán's solar export model depends on continued technology cost declines and grid infrastructure maintenance that could be disrupted by climate events.
- **Counter-scenario economic impact:** If the "clean revolution" path materialized (see [meta/counter-scenario.md](../../meta/counter-scenario.md)), a unified successor state with coordinated economic transition would have vastly stronger economic position than the fragmented scenario described here. Low probability but high impact — worth tracking as a diverging possibility space.
